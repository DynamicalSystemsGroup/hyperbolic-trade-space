## 1. Computer-aided governance (read in full)

- Title: "Computer-Aided Governance: A Revolution in Automated Decision-Support Systems". Authors: Jeff Emmett, "co-authored
  by Michael Zargham & Jeff Emmett". Venue: BlockScience blog (first on Medium). Date: 7 August 2019 (page metadata).
- URL (200): https://blog.block.science/computer-aided-governance-a-revolution-in-automated-decision-support-systems/
  (Medium original, 403 to automated requests, read through the Internet Archive snapshot of 2025-09-25).
- Bears: it defines the idea by what it is not, and keeps the decision with people: models simulate the results of
  governance policies so that the people deciding are better informed. It names Roth: market design is "pioneered by
  Alvin Roth" and is about steering systems that cannot be totally controlled by metrics of healthy states. It speaks of
  policy "leverage points" and of risks of unintended consequences, not of a unique best policy. Quote: "CAG is not
  governance by computers." It does not use "design space" and does not say the choice among alternatives is politics.
- Companions read: "Computer-Aided Governance in Action: Steering Complex Systems Using the CAG Map", Jeff Emmett with
  Jessica Zartler and Kelsie Nabben, research by Zargham and Burrrata, BlockScience blog, 17 November 2022,
  https://blog.block.science/computer-aided-governance-in-action-steering-complex-systems-using-the-cag-map/ (200).
  It says participatory decisions involve political compromise, that people read the same data differently, and that
  models are "sensemaking tools" that inform discourse rather than replace it (the quoted phrase is the unit's
  paraphrase of the page; no quotation is taken). Also the repository https://github.com/BlockScience/cag-map (200),
  README read: a work-in-progress guide, no authors or dates on the page.

## 3. Computer-aided ethnography (read in full)

- Title: "'Computer Aided Ethnography' (CAE)". Authors: Kelsie Nabben (RMIT, BlockScience), Michael Zargham
  (BlockScience), Ellie Rennie (RMIT). Venue: Nabben's Substack. Date: 20 February 2023.
- URL (200): https://kelsienabben.substack.com/p/computer-aided-ethnography-cae
- Bears: it builds explicitly on computer-aided governance (Zargham and Emmett 2019, and the 2021 re-post) and carries its
  stance over: not governance by computers, and so not research by technology. The community studied takes part on its
  own terms (the Telescope Discord bot lets members approve or refuse use of their messages), and researchers are asked
  to reflect on how their tools change the community. Quote: "CAG is not governance by computers". It says nothing on
  design spaces or on optimum versus politics.
- Nearby, read as abstracts: "Toward a Participatory Digital Ethnography of Blockchain Governance", Rennie, Zargham, Tan,
  Miller, Abbott, Nabben, De Filippi, Qualitative Inquiry 28(7), 2022, https://doi.org/10.1177/10778004221097056 (resolves
  to SAGE, 403 to automated requests; listed on Zargham's publications page).

## 4. Building the Loop (read in full: the published PDF in his Downloads, and the EPIC page)

- Title: "Building the Loop: The Role of Ethnography in Artificial Organisational Intelligence". Authors: Ellie Rennie,
  Kelsie Nabben, Michael Zargham, Jason Potts, Brooke Ann Coco, Luke Miller, Matthew Green (ARC Centre of Excellence for
  Automated Decision-Making and Society; BlockScience). Venue: EPIC Proceedings 2025(1), pp. 223-235 (the EPIC page
  and the PDF cite 224-36 and 2026; Crossref gives issued 2025-11; the EPIC page lists 19 January 2026). CC BY-NC-ND.
- DOI (200 to the EPIC page): https://doi.org/10.1111/epic.70009 ; page
  https://proceedings.epicpeople.org/article/154018-building-the-loop-the-role-of-ethnography-in-artificial-organisational-intelligence
- Bears: it moves the question from whether a human is "in the loop" at the moment of decision to how the loops are
  built: how organisations set permissions and shape the flow of knowledge across people and machines. Governance is
  treated as a group process, with rules set at each organisation's own node and no shared ontology imposed. The
  authors say plainly that they do not answer questions of liability or regulation. Quote: "not about divine moral
  authority of the human". It does not treat optimization, trade-offs in a design space or the politics of choosing
  among alternatives as themes; its bearing is on who is in the loop and how it is built.

## 5. The attention-economy paper (read: abstract and pages; the full text was not read)

- Title: "Online Governance Surfaces and Attention Economies". Authors: Nathan Schneider, Kelsie Nabben, Ronen Tamari,
  Michael Zargham (Metagov). Venue: OSF preprint on MediArXiv, published 30 August 2025 (OSF API); the Indiana Digital
  Library of the Commons holds a version as "Attention Economies and Online Governance Surfaces" (2024, handle
  https://hdl.handle.net/10535/11011, 200; the Ostrom Workshop is its listed contact, and the page does not say it was
  presented there).
- URL (200): https://osf.io/preprints/mediarxiv/cdrmp_v1 (abstract by the OSF API); announcement
  https://kelsienabben.substack.com/p/new-paper-online-governance-surfaces (200); seminar page https://luma.com/nfor1dtz (200).
- Bears: frameworks for governing community-managed resources, Ostrom's among them, give too little weight to how
  attention is engaged and directed; the paper offers heuristics that ask whether the structure of attention in a system
  is "appropriate, efficient, and just" (abstract). That is a normative question left to people, asked of a system by
  analytic heuristics. No source found places this paper at an Ostrom Workshop event in 2026; the Workshop on the Ostrom
  Workshop 7 was June 2024, and the next is not announced. Reported as not found.
- Also found, related: "Path Dependence: An Uncomfortable Institutional Design Axiom", Alston, Case, Zargham, Workshop
  on the Ostrom Workshop 7 (June 2024), https://hdl.handle.net/10535/11015 (200); abstract only. It argues that not every
  idealized institutional choice is reachable because the path to a configuration shapes what is attainable.

## 6. Computer-aided institutional design, building on Roth

- Roth, A. E. (2002), "The Economist as Engineer: Game Theory, Experimentation, and Computation as Tools for Design
  Economics", Econometrica 70(4), 1341-1378, https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-0262.00335 (Crossref confirms title, journal,
  July 2002; the DOI redirect answers 403 to automated requests). Read: the published PDF found on his machine (the
  public article), introduction and the passages on design and politics. Bears: design needs an engineering approach, may
  learn from history and tinkering, and "the adoption of a design is at least partly a political process" (Roth).
  Politicians and regulators stay involved. It does not say there is no best design; it says the adoption is political.
- Public work of his that cites Roth and builds on him, all read:
  - "Market Design Lessons for 'Safety First' Institutional Engineering", Jamsheed Shorish with Zargham and Ilan Ben-Meir,
    BlockScience blog, 25 May 2023, https://blog.block.science/market-design-lessons-for-safety-first-institutional-engineering/
    (200). Cites Roth 2002 for "economic engineering"; treats designs as trading qualitative goals (fairness, do no harm)
    against quantitative measures (match rates); has designers identify reachable states by theory and simulation, name
    a "governance surface" by which stakeholders adjust the system, and optimize only after safety is shown. It does not
    use "design space" or say that the choice is politics.
  - "Model-Based Institutional Design", Zargham, BlockScience blog, 1 April 2023,
    https://blog.block.science/model-based-institutional-design/ (200): pairs institutional design with computer-aided
    governance as human participation with tools; does not mention Roth, trade-offs or politics.
  - "Protocols and Institutions", Zargham and Ben-Meir, Zenodo 15122312, 6 March 2025,
    https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15122312 (resolves to Zenodo, 403 to automated requests): abstract only; lists
    market design among the fields it draws on; does not mention Roth.
- Not found: any public text of his that uses "computer-aided institutional design" as a phrase, or that states "a unique
  optimum versus a design space whose choice is politics". The lineage in the supervisor's direction is his own framing;
  the sources above carry its parts (humans stay in charge, models inform and do not decide, adoption of a design is
  political, trade-offs between goals) but no one of them states it whole.
