## 1. What this is about

It is a method for analysing a table of designs, each already evaluated into numbers, so that a trade study becomes a model and not a spreadsheet. The method fits a surface to the outer edge of the data. It is a posynomial in the "metrics", where a metric is a conditioned attribute: strictly positive, larger is better. The surface comes with a coordinate system. Each design gets a position along the surface and a "level" (log V) relative to it. Each term of the fit names the metrics that contend, with elasticities (percent of one metric given up per percent of another gained). It is positioned beside PCA (it does a different job and composes with it), Pareto analysis, and data envelopment analysis. A cubesat model with 13 metrics serves as the worked case.

For my purpose (I build the system model that produces the table), the paper treats me as "stage 1": configuration c to attributes a. It says the evaluation is a black box (simulator, physical test, human assessment). Conditioning attributes into metrics (stage 2) is the analyst's job. Stages 3 and 4, fit and analysis, are the method.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- Define V as a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled by an offset beta so that V = 1 on the fitted surface. Doubling every metric doubles V. log V is the "energy differential", the level of a design relative to the surface. The weights w sit on the simplex.
- The one-term (K = 1) fit minimises a log-mean-exp (soft maximum) of the scores w.u_i over the simplex. This is convex in w, so any minimiser is global. Uniqueness is not claimed. The exponents are elasticities.
- The chart (Z, log V) is a reversible rewriting of the log-metrics. Z is the position along the surface, free of overall scale. Under log-affine conditioning the pull-back to attribute units is exact.
- A maximiser of V over a set is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient if every exponent is positive. The converse is not claimed. The surface is explicitly not the Pareto boundary.
- For several terms (K > 1) the objective is not convex. The paper offers an alternating fit with three levels of guarantee: descent, a numerical stationarity "gap", and local optimality when an assignment margin is positive. A soft fit has a second-order certificate. Each returned fit states which level it meets.
- Four questions are open: global convergence, statistical consistency, identifiability under finite and biased samples, and when the surface recovers the Pareto boundary rather than the supporting envelope of the sample.
- On the cubesat case, one term blends the subsystems' trade-offs, and two terms separate the contending sets. The paper reports that dominance stops shortlisting here: the nondominated set nears the whole table.
- The set of contending metrics (the n-lemma) depends on the conditioning, so it is an answer to the question the analyst posed, not a context-free discovery.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

"It fits a soft upper envelope to a table of outcomes in log space and tells you which metrics trade against which, at what exchange rate, in each region. It does not rank designs or find the true Pareto front. Every result depends on how you condition your outputs, and the maths only holds given assumptions you must judge yourself. The one-term version is a clean convex problem. The multi-term version is local and comes with a declared level of certification."

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It does not do conditioning. That stays with the analyst.
- It does not predict. There is no `predict`. Prediction composes a regressor with the chart.
- It does not give causal exchange rates. The exponents describe how the sampled designs trade, not what changing a variable would do.
- The paper does not claim that the surface is the Pareto boundary, that the fit holds for designs outside the table, that it supports a decision (a third claim that "needs its own validation"), or that a maximiser's efficiency has a converse.
- It does not claim global convergence, consistency, identifiability, or that tensions are orthogonal. It does not claim that successive-tension extraction works.
- It claims no novelty and no priority, and does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis.
- The search section (Section 7) is declared future work with no results. The kernel direction is not built.
- It does not handle discrete variables beyond finite comparisons, and does not detect dents or holes in the envelope.
- It does not select a design. That falls to experts.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I guessed

- "Metric": a conditioned attribute, strictly positive, larger is better. I got this from Section 3 and was sure.
- "Conditioning": the rescaling of attributes into metrics (log-affine, with sign flip for smaller-is-better and a shift). I guessed the scaler is a standard scaler on the log values.
- "Energy differential" / "level": log V. Clear after Section 4; the analogy to potential is explicitly not physical.
- "Reach" (beta): the soft maximum of the scores along the term direction. I guessed it is the offset that places the surface at V = 1.
- "Near-frontier band": designs with |log V| < 1/tau.
- "n-lemma": the set of metrics contending in a term. I treated "dilemma, trilemma" as the analogy.
- "Contending set": the support (non-negligible exponents) of the exact-maximum fit to the term's cell. "Cell" I guessed to be the set of designs for which the term is the largest.
- "Participation threshold": the exponent size above which a metric counts as in the term. Which threshold, see item 14.
- "Assignment margin", "stable radius", "term gap": guessed as margins of how clear each design's assignment to a term is, and how close the fit is to the first-order optimum.
- "Sharp weights" / "soft weights": hard versus softmax assignment of designs to terms.
- "Hole": a region of the chart with no designs, a candidate opportunity.
- "Supporting envelope": the tau = infinity surface which no table design exceeds.
- "Executives' measures": the pointing error, unit cost and fleet cost (the MoEs).
- "Regime": the aggressive-gains versus stock-gains datasets.
- "Hyperbolic": the level curves m1 m2 = const when exponents are equal.
- "Posynomial extension", "GGP": I know geometric programming; I took GGP to be generalised geometric programming.

## 8. Questions I am left with

- What exactly must my model supply, and in what form (a table with columns for configurations and attributes, how many rows, how many metrics), and what happens at the minimum sizes? The paper says "small samples determine exponents weakly" and does not say how small.
- How should I choose conditioning, in particular scale and shift, so the answer is not an artefact? Is there a sensitivity check provided?
- How do I choose K, other than by reading the figures? Section 12 lists "selection rules" as future work.
- How robust are the contending sets to noise in my attributes, given that Q2 is open?
- If some of my outputs are exactly zero for a set of designs (failures), how many rows can be lost before the fit changes?
- What does the paper count as agreement between the fit and the Pareto boundary in the case? Q4 is open, and the case has no ground-truth comparison, except for planted data.
- How much of the case's headline (one term blends, two terms separate) depends on the choices of tau (10 percent) and the conditioning? Appendix D.5 is cited for tau.
- What is the "dominance stops shortlisting" number?
- Does the K = 2 separation correspond to something a subsystem engineer would recognise? The paper says that is human validation.
- How are design variables that are discrete, like wheel class, handled in the ascent direction? The paper treats variables as continuous within a category.
