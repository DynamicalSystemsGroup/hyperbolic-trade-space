## 1. What it is about

It is a way to turn a table of evaluated designs (each row a configuration, each column a measured outcome, all conditioned to be positive and larger-is-better) into a fitted "reference surface". The surface sits at the outer edge of the data, not through the middle. It is built from one or more power-law terms (a product of metrics raised to exponents that sum to one). Each term reports which metrics trade off against each other and at what rate (the exponents read as elasticities). Every design also gets a coordinate pair: where it sits along the surface, and how far inside or beyond the surface it is. The pitch is that this replaces weighted-criteria grids with something reproducible that an executive can follow. It is offered as an alternative to PCA for trade studies. PCA says where the data vary, and this says what trades against what. A cubesat model with thirteen metrics is the worked example.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- With one term, you find exponents w (non-negative, summing to 1) that minimise a soft maximum of the weighted log-metrics over the designs. The softness is set by a temperature. This is convex, so any minimiser is global, though it need not be unique. The surface is where the weighted geometric mean V, rescaled by an offset, equals 1. Designs with V above 1 lie beyond it. The paper sets the temperature so that about 10 percent of designs lie beyond.
- The exponents give elasticities: along a term, a 1 percent gain in metric i costs w_i/w_j percent of metric j. V doubles when all metrics double.
- The chart (position along the surface, log V) is an invertible rewriting of the log-metrics. The pull-back to the original attribute units is exact if the conditioning is log-affine.
- A maximiser of V over a set of designs is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient if all exponents are positive. The converse is not claimed, and the surface is explicitly not the Pareto boundary.
- With several terms the fit is not convex. It is done by alternating assignment and refitting. The paper gives three levels of guarantee: the objective does not rise, a measurable stationarity gap, and local optimality when the assignment margin is positive. There is a second-order certificate for the soft variant. Global convergence, consistency under noise, identifiability, and recovery of the true Pareto boundary are listed as open (Q1 to Q4).
- On the cubesat, one term gives a single 8-metric tension, and two terms split into contending sets of 8 and 7 metrics (the Figure 7 panel titles). The case table shows a baseline with level -0.330 and candidate designs chosen by rules, with levels near zero.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

Method does not: pick a design or rank designs (Table 2's levels are "not a ranking"); find the Pareto boundary; give a causal exchange rate (elasticities describe the sampled designs); predict outside the table without separate validation; handle categorical variables beyond continuous-within-category; choose K or the conditioning for you; give uncertainty on the exponents (designed samples such as grids are not independent observations); detect tensions the sampling never exercised; schedule or risk.

Paper does not claim: novelty or priority ("claims nothing as novel"); that it replaces PCA or Pareto analysis; global convergence of the multi-term fit; that tensions are orthogonal or successive; uniqueness of the minimiser; that any hole in the chart is an opportunity; that the search/design-of-experiments section has results (it is "future work with no results"); that the kernel direction is built.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- "Posynomial": I guessed a sum of products of metrics raised to real powers with positive coefficients. The paper's gloss ("a sum of monomial terms") matches. Whether the model is a sum or a maximum at the default setting I had to guess (maximum).
- "Reach" (β): I guessed the offset: how high the data go along the term's direction.
- "Energy differential": a design's log V, its distance inside or beyond the surface in log units.
- "Position along the surface" (Z): the d-1 coordinates left after removing overall scale. I guessed a coordinate system on the log-metric simplex-like space.
- "Contending set" and "n-lemma": the metrics with non-negligible exponent in a term, and the count of them.
- "Elasticity": percent change per percent change, in conditioned metrics.
- "Near-frontier band": designs within 1/τ of the surface in log V.
- "Term share": fraction of near-frontier designs whose largest term is this one.
- "Cell": the designs that a term scores highest.
- "Balanced point": the softmax-weighted mean of log-metrics.
- "Mean slack": the average gap between surface and design.
- "Effective sample size": guessed as 1 over the sum of squared normalised soft weights.
- "Support" (of an exponent vector): the metrics with non-zero exponent.
- "Participation threshold": the cutoff for printing/reading an exponent as non-zero.
- "Hole" (in the chart): a region without designs inside the sampled range.
- "Four-metric frontier/chart": a chart restricted to four metrics; never introduced.
- "Dents and holes": non-log-convex features of the data that a log-convex envelope would miss.
- "Primary regime" and "baseline regime": two datasets, aggressive controller gains and stock gains.
- "MoE" and "MoP": measures of effectiveness (stakeholder-facing) and of performance (subsystem-level); the paper defines these.
- "Planted" tables: synthetic tables where the true terms are set by the analyst, used to test recovery.
- "Generalized posynomial", "GGP": from geometric programming; I took them to mean a class that a convex solver can handle.
- "Weakly Pareto-efficient": nothing is better in every metric at once.
- "Unconditioned/ conditioned" attributes and "scaler": the standard preprocessing.
- "Certified", "stationary": the status labels of a fit; I guessed certified is the strongest.
- "Sharp" and "soft" weights: hard assignment of rows to the highest term, or smooth weighting.
- "Hyperbolic": the hyperbola m1 m2 = const.

## 8. Questions that remain

- How many designs and metrics does the cubesat table have, and what fraction is nondominated (the claim that dominance stops shortlisting)?

- How do I choose K, and how stable are the terms across restarts, samples and K?
- Do the fits of the case meet the margin and gap conditions, and which level of certificate do the reported fits carry?
- How do the eight- and seven-metric contending sets at K = 2 relate to the five-metric set at K = 1, and what makes the terms engineering-meaningful rather than numerical splits?
- What would I show in a design review to justify a recommended design, given that levels are not a ranking and the paper says decision support needs its own validation?
