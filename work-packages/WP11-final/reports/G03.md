# G03
Head: c23cf60

## 1. What it is about

It proposes a way to read a trade-study table as a fitted surface. The table holds designs, each evaluated into metrics where larger is better. The author fits a posynomial "reference surface" to the outer edge of the data, the edge a few designs may slightly exceed, rather than through the middle of the cloud. Each design then gets two numbers: a position along that surface, and a level above or below it (called the energy differential, a log offset). Each monomial term of the surface names the set of metrics that trade against each other on that piece (an "n-lemma", by analogy with dilemma and trilemma), and gives the trade rates (elasticities). The paper presents this as an alternative to a weighted-criteria table and to Pareto filtering, and positions it against PCA, DEA, frontier estimation, geometric programming and HoroPCA. The worked case is a cubesat model with thirteen traded metrics, 3,000 random designs per regime, and about 2,134 feasible designs in the primary regime.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- The fitted surface is the level set where a weighted geometric mean of the metrics (the "invariant value" V) equals one. It is not the Pareto boundary; the paper says it models the data, and separately says what the Pareto boundary would be (Definition 1, and the Theorem 1 and Theorem 5 material).
- With one term the fit is a geometric program in the log variables. Every minimizer is global, but uniqueness is not claimed. The chart (position and level) is an invertible rewriting of the log-metrics, and the exponents are the elasticities on the surface.
- With one term, a maximizer of V is weakly Pareto-efficient. The converse, that every efficient design is reached, is not claimed.
- With several terms the fit is not convex. The paper gives three levels of guarantee: descent of the objective under an assumption about unique term minimizers, numerical stationarity measured by a term gap, and local optimality only when the assignment margin is positive. A second-order certificate covers the soft fit. Global convergence is left open.
- In the case, K = 1 gives one tension with seven contending metrics. K = 2 separates two terms with contending sets of eight and seven metrics, with near-frontier design shares of 0.74 and 0.26. Two metrics (power margin and battery peak power ratio) enter no contending set, because each is almost one axis with another metric.
- The paper's own certificate numbers are weak at K = 2: the assignment margin is far smaller than the band, so about 37 of the 2,134 designs sit near a seam and their term assignment is called "not a finding".
- For the decision, the requirements and the frontier reduce 2,134 feasible designs to 28, and the executives' open question (whether a fine star tracker is worth about 0.56 M USD of a 6 M USD fleet limit) is left to them.

## 3. What I would do with it next

I would take one table of my own with a known answer, plant terms in it (the paper does this with a generator), and check whether the fit recovers them before I trust a real table. I would look at how the fit temperature moves the contending sets, because the paper's figure shows the exponents depend on it, and I would test the K choice on my data rather than accept K = 2. I would also make my conditioning choices explicit and see how much the n-lemmas move when they change. The Pareto part I would check against the nondominated set I already compute. If I connected it to my side, the first thing I would need from my system model is a clean definition of which outcomes are larger-is-better and which are costs, because the sign of the conditioning drives everything.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

It replaces a weighted trade table with a fitted, reversible map of the sampled designs. It is useful when Pareto filtering stops discriminating, because the map says which metrics trade and at what rate, and it gives each design a signed distance to the edge. It is a fit, not a causal model, and it says nothing about designs outside the table. The cubesat example is worth reading for the mechanics, and the author is explicit that the interpretation is for a human engineer.

## 5. What I believe the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It does not give a causal exchange rate. The exponents describe how sampled designs trade.
- It does not establish global convergence of the several-term alternation (Q1), statistical consistency under noise (Q2), identifiability of active metrics under biased observations (Q3), or that the surface recovers the Pareto boundary rather than the supporting envelope of the sample (Q4).
- It does not give sampling error. Grid-like samples are not independent, and the paper says so.
- It does not validate the hypotheses for a given table. The paper says that is human validation, and that the choice of K, the conditioning and every reading are the analyst's.
- The soft-fit certificate and the sharp-fit certificates cover only local statements, under tolerances and under radius conditions.
- The proposal and prediction part is a check on a few proposals, not an estimate of any rate, and it does not predict feasibility.
- It does not claim novelty for its pieces, and it does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis.
- It does not say anything about schedule, risk, the value of a larger fleet, or what to buy beyond the modelled parts.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess, with my guesses

- n-lemma: the set of metrics that contend on one term, named by its size, like dilemma (two) or trilemma (three).
- contending set: the metrics with a non-zero exponent in the exact-maximum fit on the term's cell.
- elasticity: the percent change in one metric per percent change in another along a term, with a sign and a ratio of exponents.
- invariant value V: a scaled weighted geometric mean of the metrics, with V = 1 on the fitted surface.
- energy differential: the log of V, a design's level above or below the surface, read like a potential offset.
- reach beta: the soft maximum of the scores along a term's weight direction, which sets where the surface sits.
- term share: the fraction of near-frontier designs in one term's cell.
- cell: the designs whose largest term is that term.
- fit temperature tau and term temperature kappa: softness parameters of the soft maximum; larger means harder.
- near-frontier band: designs with |log V| below 1/tau.
- participation threshold, support threshold, numerically zero: three cut-offs on exponent size, each at a different level.
- mean slack F: the average over designs of how far the surface lies above each design, which the fit minimizes.
- term gap G_k: a duality-style gap that is zero exactly at the simplex stationarity conditions.
- assignment margin gamma and stable radius r: the gap between the best and second-best term score, and the size of perturbation under which assignments cannot change.
- effective sample size: the inverse sum of squared softmax weights over designs, a count of designs carrying the fit.
- certified, stationary: status labels from the solver, which the paper says are measured.
- F5, F10, F12, I14, I15: hypothesis codes for fallback and inherited results.
- MoE and MoP: measure of effectiveness (read by the stakeholder) and measure of performance (read off subsystems).
- hole, dent: a region of the chart with no designs, and a local departure of the surface.
- kernel direction: some kernel expansion of the features that keeps the fit a geometric program.
- weakly efficient: no design is better in every metric at once.
- the package: the software that implements the method.

## 8. Questions that remain for me

- Does the 7-lemma of the primary K = 1 fit exclude total mass by design, and what does the paper call a metric that has an exponent at the fit temperature but is not in the n-lemma?
- Which band and which temperature apply to each reported figure, the proposal check, and the K = 2 certificate?
- How are the thirteen attributes turned into the metrics that are fitted, and is any one dropped?
- Are the six requirements the only requirements, given the figure?
- Is the 2,134 nondominated count over the feasible set or the full table?
- What is the monotonicity question of the search, stated precisely?
- What exactly does "machine-checked" cover, and what does "proved" cover?
- How robust is the choice of K = 2, and what is the planted-table recovery rate on tables like the case's?
- Would a designed sample (a grid) change the contending sets, given the paper says grid samples are not independent?
