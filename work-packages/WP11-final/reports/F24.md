## 1. What this is about

A way to read a table of evaluated designs as a trade study. Each design is a configuration evaluated into attributes, which are conditioned into strictly positive metrics that are larger-is-better. The method fits a posynomial "reference surface" to the outer edge of the metrics' log-table, using a soft maximum with a temperature. It then reports (a) a reversible chart of each design's position along the surface and its level relative to it (the "energy differential"), and (b) for each monomial term, a set of contending metrics (an "n-lemma") with elasticities read from its exponents. The case is a cubesat model (power, mass, attitude control) with 3,000 random designs per regime, 2,134 of them feasible in the primary regime. The paper claims a reproducible, scalable reading of trade-offs, not a new Pareto method, and says so.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- The invariant value V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, so doubling every metric doubles it. The surface is V = 1 (Theorem 1 and Definition 2). Level curves at equal weights are the hyperbolas that give the title's word.
- One term: the fit minimises a log-mean-exp of a linear score over the simplex. At tau = infinity it is a linear program; at finite tau it is a geometric program, convex in the exponents (Theorems 2 to 4). The exponents are elasticities (Theorem 5), and the chart is an invertible rewriting (Theorems 6 and 7).
- Several terms: the posynomial is the 1/kappa power of a sum of monomials, and its log is within (log K)/kappa of its largest term (Theorem 8). The sharp-weight alternation has three levels of guarantee: descent, numerical stationarity by a term gap, and local optimality under an assumption margin (Theorem 11, Lemma 1, Lemma 2, Proposition 3). A soft fit has a second-order test (Theorem 12).
- Pareto: a maximiser of V is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient when every exponent is positive (Theorem 9). The converse is not claimed.
- Case: one term blends the trade-offs into a 7-metric contending set. At K = 2 the cells split into two terms with 8 and 7 contending metrics. The decision question (fine star tracker versus a cheaper fleet) is priced and left to the executives.

What I believe is established: the one-term algebra (homogeneity, shift, the chart, the Pareto implication), the convexity of the one-term objective, and the descent and local-optimality statements under their stated hypotheses. The case numbers I take as measured on that table, not as general findings. The paper says the same, in Section 6 and Section 10.

## 3. What you would do next with it

I would run it on one of my own tables with a known efficient frontier, to see whether the envelope recovers the boundary or the envelope of the sample (the paper's open question Q4). I would also look at what happens to the contending set when a dominated design is added or removed, since the paper's claim that dominance "stops discriminating" is a claim about this table. I would want the Appendix D sensitivity results first, to see how far the n-lemma survives the choice of tau.

## 4. What you would tell a colleague

It is a one-sided, soft envelope fit with an exponent per term, and the honest selling point is the readout: a term's contending metrics and its elasticities. That is useful to an engineer and is not in DEA or the usual frontier estimators, which give weights per unit or a boundary set by extreme units. The fit is convex for one term, and the paper does not oversell the several-term part. The main thing a colleague should know is that the surface is a convention-laden object: the choice of tau (a 10 percent target fraction above the surface), the conditioning, and K all set the answer, and the paper says so.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It does not give a global optimum for several terms (Q1), or consistency under noise (Q2), or identifiability under biased samples (Q3). The paper states these as open.
- It does not recover the Pareto boundary. It fits a one-sided envelope of the sample (Q4 and Section 2, "Pareto methods").
- It gives no causal reading. The Discussion says that exponents describe the sampled designs, not what changing a variable would do.
- It does not say whether a design above the surface is worth anything ("Novelty V-1 says how far beyond ... not whether anyone wants it").
- The paper does not claim novelty for its parts ("The method claims nothing as novel"), and says the combination in one estimator is its design (Section 2, Lineage).

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- "Energy differential": I guessed log V, the level of a design relative to the surface (negative inside, positive beyond). The text confirms this.
- "n-lemma": I guessed the number of contending metrics in a term, by analogy with dilemma and trilemma. The text confirms it, with the count set by the threshold in item 8.
- "Participation threshold": I guessed it is the exponent level below which a metric is not counted. Two values are given (10^-9 for the support, 10^-3 for participation), so I could not settle which one the paper means by the word.
- "Ridge" (Section 5, "kinks on the ridges"): I guessed the non-smooth points of a max over terms. I could not confirm this.
- "Reach" and "term share" (Definition 7): I read the reach as a soft maximum of the scores along a term's direction, and the share as the fraction of near-frontier designs in a term's cell. Both match the text.
- "Band" (1/tau): the paper defines it as |log V| < 1/tau. I used this throughout. The "regressor's own band" in Section 9 is the one term I could not place.
