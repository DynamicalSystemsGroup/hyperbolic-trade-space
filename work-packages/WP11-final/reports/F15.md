## 1. What this is about

A method, "hyperbolic trade space" (HTS), for taking a table of candidate designs with measured outcomes and fitting a curved surface that describes the trade-offs among the outcomes. The outcomes are first turned into positive "bigger is better" numbers (metrics). The surface is a frontier of the form "product of metrics, raised to powers, equals a constant", so getting more of one metric costs you some of another. It is offered as the trade-study analogue of PCA: unsupervised, fit to the data, and implemented as a scikit-learn estimator. A cubesat study with thirteen metrics is the worked example.

## 2. The main results, stated as I understand them

- You fit a surface one dimension below the data (a frontier among the metrics). Each design is then described by where it sits along the surface and by its "energy", the level above or below the surface. The abstract says this loses nothing, so I read it as invertible: you can go back to the original design.
- With a single product term (monomial) the fit is a convex optimisation problem. With several terms (posynomial) it fits within generalized geometric programming.
- A single term in n metrics is called an "n-lemma", a generalised dilemma or trilemma. In the cubesat example, high-dimensional n-lemmas were found and used to inform a cost versus performance decision.
- Raising energy is a Pareto improvement, and a design with maximal energy is weakly Pareto-efficient.
- Empty regions inside an occupied level set may be under-explored parts of the design space. A point with no data is a conjecture about a design, to be settled by building and measuring it.
- The proofs are SymPy scripts, and the code is a scikit-learn estimator.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

From the abstract only:
- It does not do the conditioning for you in any way I can see. Conditioning is a step that happens first, and its choices affect everything after.
- It does not say how to choose among competing fits, or how many terms to use.
- It does not claim the frontier is the true physical limit of the design space. It assumes log-convexity of the frontier. I do not know what happens when that fails.
- It does not generate designs. Searching and sampling of white space is listed as future work.
- No claim is made about formal verification. That is also future work.
- It makes no claim about weights, stakeholder preferences or how to pick a final design. The decision is "informed", not made.
- Nothing is said about uncertainty in the measurements or in the fit.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- Attribute versus metric: attribute is a raw outcome measurement, metric is the same made positive and bigger-is-better.
- Conditioning: transformation and scaling of attributes (and probably encoding of categorical ones) into metrics.
- Posynomial: a sum of products of variables raised to real powers with positive coefficients (I know the term loosely from geometric programming).
- Monomial case: a single product term.
- Log-convex frontier: convex when the axes are log metrics.
- Level set: the set of designs with the same value of the posynomial.
- Energy: the value of that function, or its log. The abstract uses it as a score where higher is better.
- Weakly Pareto-efficient: no design is strictly better on all metrics at once.
- n-lemma: a trade-off among n metrics bound together in one term.
- White space: regions of metric space inside the frontier's range with no designs.
- Pulls back: maps a point on the trade space back to the source design.
