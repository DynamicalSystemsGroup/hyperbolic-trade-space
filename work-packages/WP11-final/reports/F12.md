## 1. What this is about

The paper proposes a way to summarise a trade study's table of evaluated designs. Each design is a vector of metrics (larger is better, strictly positive). The method fits a posynomial "reference surface" to the outer edge of the data, using a soft maximum at a chosen temperature, and then reads the trade-offs off that fit: each monomial term names a set of metrics that contend (an "n-lemma"), with elasticities as exponents. A "chart" re-expresses each design as a position along the surface plus a level relative to it. The worked case is a cubesat model (power, mass, attitude control, cost) with 3,000 random designs per regime, of which 2,134 are feasible in the primary regime. The author says plainly that the method claims nothing as novel and does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- The invariant value V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled so the fitted surface is V = 1 (Definition 2, Theorem 1). Homogeneity and the shift identities are straightforward.
- For one term, the fit is convex in the log variables (Theorem 3: the second derivative along any line is tau times a variance, so nonnegative), so any minimiser is global. Uniqueness is not claimed. I checked the Hessian formula and the convexity argument and they hold as written.
- Log-mean-exp versus log-sum-exp (Theorem 4) is a correct identity with the stated bounds.
- With one term the elasticity and chart results (Theorems 5 and 6) hold; the derivative identity -w_i/w_j is correct on the hyperplane.
- A maximiser of V with positive exponents is weakly Pareto-efficient (Theorem 9). The argument is sound, and the converse is explicitly not claimed.
- For several terms, the fit is not convex. The paper gives three levels of guarantee for the sharp alternation: descent (Theorem 11), a numerical term-gap stationarity measure (Lemma 1, which I checked: 0 <= f(w) - f* <= G(w) follows from convexity), and conditional local optimality with a radius (Lemma 2, Proposition 3, Corollary 2). I checked Lemma 2's radius arithmetic and the Proposition 3 logic and they hold given the stated hypotheses. The soft fit gets a second-order certificate (Theorem 12), which is conditional on positive definiteness.
- Four questions are open (Q1 global convergence, Q2 consistency under noise, Q3 identifiability, Q4 recovery of the Pareto boundary versus the supporting envelope). The paper says so.
- On the case, the K = 2 numbers are internally consistent where I checked them: the band of 0.0088 corresponds to tau = 113; the two-term half-margin is about 47 times smaller than the band; the Table 2 fleet costs equal fleet size times unit cost.

## 3. What I would do next with it

- Work through the finite-stop argument for the sharp alternation (Theorem 11). Strict descent is needed for finiteness; the page says weak descent plus unique minimisers and a fixed tie rule, and I could not see from the main text how a non-strict step is excluded.
- Check the sign split of the soft-fit Hessian (H = D - kappa M, with M claimed positive semidefinite) against the appendix derivation, since the second-order certificate rests on it.
- Reconcile the ridge constant in Corollary 1 with Proposition 2 (see section 6).
- Test the one-term uniqueness claim (R1 at tau = infinity) directly, since the exact-maximum fit is a linear program and uniqueness of its optimum is not generally guaranteed.
- Replicate the planted-table recovery on a larger grid of planted terms, and check the "about 10 percent above V = 1" convention on the K = 2 chart, which I could not see.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

It is a convex-analysis-based trade-off fit with honest guarantees: the one-term case is a clean geometric program, and the several-term case is a non-convex alternation with a stated, partly conditional, stationarity story. The author separates derived, numerical and open statements in the results section, which is more careful than most applied papers. The useful idea is the chart: a position along a fitted surface plus a level, so that the trade-offs read as exponents. The mathematics I checked is correct, but the numerical "certified" status and the "machine-checked" language should be read as measured, not proved.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

The paper does not claim that the fitted surface is the Pareto boundary; it says the surface models the data and that recovering the boundary is open (Q4). It does not claim convergence of the several-term alternation, statistical consistency, identifiability of active metrics, or that exponents are causal (Discussion: "not a causal exchange"). It does not claim the fit holds outside the table, and it does not support a decision: the cubesat fleet choice is left to the executives. Designed grids are said not to be independent observations, so the exponents are not estimates with a sampling error. It does not cover tensions that the sampling never exercised.

## 6. Where I was confused, stopped or found something imprecise (in reading order)

1. Section 1 Main results: "a maximizer of V is weakly Pareto-efficient (Theorem 8, Theorem 9)". The claim is Theorem 9; Theorem 8 is the posynomial extension. A citation mismatch.
2. Section 2, Table 1, Geometric programming row: the reference Candes et al. 2008 ("Enhancing Sparsity by Reweighted l1 Minimization") is listed as a source for geometric programming and max-affine fitting. I could not see why it belongs to that family.
3. Section 2, "The title's 'hyperbolic' is defined in Section 4 and Appendix A." Definition 2 and the following text say the level curves are a rectangular hyperbola only when the two weights are equal. The title names the method after that special case. I would like to know whether the name is meant to be general.
4. Section 3, the thresholds. "An exponent above the participation threshold (10-3) participates. One at or below 10-12 is numerically zero." Definition 6 defines the contending set as "the support (exponents above 10-9)". Three thresholds appear and their roles are not reconciled on the page.
5. Section 5, Simplifying assumptions (iv): "Small K: the case needs three terms." Later the case is read at K = 2, and the text says K = 2 is chosen by the package's checks. I could not tell whether "needs three terms" is a leftover or a claim.
6. Definition 3: "The package's mean (kappa = infinity: the maximum) subtracts the constant (log K)/kappa from every level." The formula immediately above is a sum (a log-sum-exp), not a mean. The sentence is confusing about which aggregation the package uses.
7. Theorem 6, the chart: the displayed relation log V = w.B^T Z + l - beta is the K = 1 form, and the K > 1 claim is asserted in brackets without the shift-equivariance argument.
8. Corollary 1 and Proposition 2. Proposition 2 gives (1/tau) log E exp(tau w.eps) = tau sigma^2 ||w||^2 / 2. Corollary 1 says the noise "adds the ridge tau^2 w' Sigma w / 2 to J_tau". Taking Proposition 2 at face value, the ridge in J_tau is (tau/2) w' Sigma w, not (tau^2/2). The Hessian statement "tau C" later is consistent with the tau version. I read this as a probable typo, but I could not reconcile it from the page.
9. Theorem 2 and the balanced point. "R1 is a statement about the exact-maximum geometry; it does not imply that the finite-tau minimizer is interior." This is honest, and I checked the KKT derivation; it holds.
10. Theorem 10 (i): "the fit's objective is symmetric but, in an instance (Appendix B, L16), lower at distinct terms than at the symmetric point, so it is not convex." The argument is a counterexample, correctly stated.
11. Section 6 intro: "Derived means proved from its hypotheses by a plain-SymPy script the tests run." A SymPy script that checks an identity or a worked instance is not a proof from general hypotheses, and the page itself says "the sketch is the idea, the script the check." The word "proved" is stronger than the evidence shown.
12. Theorem 11: "the sharp alternation stops after finitely many iterations." Weak descent does not by itself give finiteness; I could not see the strictness argument (it is pointed to Appendix B, L15.17 to L15.22, which I did not open).
13. Section 6.2, numerical status: "the solver declares the status certified at K = 2 and 3, with the largest term gap below 1e-12." The word "certified" here is a software label. The same section says that stationarity within tolerance "is measured, not proved." The Discussion then calls the optimizer result "the certificate". I would keep the three apart.
14. Section 6.2, the K = 2 numbers: "a margin below the band puts at least one design within the band of a seam, here 37 of the 2,134". Clear, and the arithmetic checks.
15. Theorem 12: the Hessian is written H = D - kappa M with D and M positive semidefinite, and the text says kappa M is "the designs' ambiguity between terms". A positive definite D - kappa M is a stronger requirement than D being positive definite, and the sign of the M term is asserted rather than shown on the page. I could not verify it from the main text.
16. Section 7: "Outward monotonicity is stated for the exact maximum and not for the soft one. Neither maximum has a lemma here." I could not find where it is stated. A claim stated but with no lemma is unclear. Also "the procedure resembles a diffusion with drift along the tangent spaces" is an analogy with no mathematics behind it on the page.
17. Section 8: "The design variables are the reaction-wheel class ... and the fleet size, a count that is no part and is not drawn." A design variable that is not drawn, and is also said to be not part of the model, is hard to read.
18. Section 8: "four thresholds ... whose limits are read from the data's pass fractions, not a mission's." The requirements are then set from the data they are later applied to. This may be intended, but it reads as circular.
19. Section 9, one term: "R1 of Section 5 holds on both regimes: the exact-maximum optimum is one point, so the support is not a solver's choice." Uniqueness of the exact-maximum optimum is asserted, not argued. R1 is a condition, and whether it holds for a table is a numerical check.
20. Section 9, Figure 5 and its caption. The caption says the dark points (other metrics near the median) "reach the line and stop there, a few beyond it". In the rendered figure, most dark points sit visibly below the line, some by about one log unit on the vertical axis, and the caption's "the line is the surface to within 0.1 in log V" is hard to square with that spread. I could not resolve it from the figure.
21. Section 9, "Two terms": the caption and text say "a tenth of all designs lie above V = 1" for the one-term case. In Figure 6 (K = 2) I could see almost no designs above the zero line; the text says each K uses the same 10 percent rule. I could not confirm the fraction from the figure.
22. Section 9, the regressor check: "the regressor's own band 1/tau = 0.0935". A tau for the regressor is never introduced; the band is different from the 0.0240 and 0.0088 used earlier.

Section 9 says the predicted position lies within the band for 3 of 3, with errors of 0.2 to 0.8 bands. 28. Discussion, "Machine verification shows that a formula follows from its hypotheses and that the code agrees". Symbolic checks of instances do not show that a formula follows from general hypotheses. Section 13 says "The derivations are machine-checked." Both phrases overstate what a SymPy script is.
29. Section 2, Table 1 and the reference list. I did not find in the main text any citation of Ahmadi-Javid 2012, Hirshorn et al. 2017, or Parnell et al. 2021, all of which are in Works cited. They may be cited in the supporting material; I could not check that.
30. The word "lemma" is used for the n-lemma (a term's contending-set concept, named by analogy with dilemma), for numbered Lemmas 1 and 2 in Section 6, and for the script labels L1 to L20. In the same paragraphs a reader meets "Lemma 2" as a mathematical result and "2-lemma" as a set size. Naming a one-metric term (the corner in Figure 1) is also left unstated.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess, and my guesses

- n-lemma: the set of metrics that contend in one term; the n is the number of such metrics (dilemma for 2, trilemma for 3).
- Energy differential: log V, the signed level of a design relative to the surface; I took it as an offset, not a physical energy, as the paper says.
- Reach (beta): the soft-maximum score of the data along a term's direction, the constant that places the surface.
- Term share: the fraction of near-frontier designs assigned to a term.
- Participation threshold: the exponent size below which a metric is treated as not in the term; I took 10-3 to be the operative one.
- Hole (in the chart): a region with no designs, which a proposal is aimed at.
- Band (1/tau): the half-width of the near-surface region, which I read as a fraction of the level scale.
- Cell: the set of designs whose largest term is a given term.
- Label, K, soft fit, sharp fit: the number of terms, the assignment rule, and the temperature regime; I took each from context.
- Executives: the case's two stakeholders, the operator and the sponsor.

## 9. Whether I would go on to read the appendices

Yes, selectively. The main text leaves its strongest claims to appendix pointers: the finite-stop argument (Appendix B, L15.17 to L15.22), the soft-fit Hessian (L17), the uniqueness and R1 checks, the numerical certification and its tolerances (Appendix D.11 and the gaps ledger in Appendix E), the regressor's tau, and the proposal rule. I would read Appendix B for the proof-step index, Appendix E for the gaps ledger, and D.11 for the robustness of the digits, because a reader who checks the mathematics cannot judge the sharp and soft guarantees, or the numerical claims, without them. I would not read the rest of the supporting material on a first pass, since the main text says what each part is for and the cubesat numbers do not affect the mathematics I checked.
