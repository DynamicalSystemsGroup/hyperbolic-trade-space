## 1. What it is about

It is a method for reading a trade-study table. You have N candidate designs, each scored on d metrics. The metrics are strictly positive and larger is better. The method fits a smooth upper-edge surface to the log of that table. The surface is made of one or more "terms", each a product of powers of the metrics.

Each term lists which metrics contend on it. It gives a percent-for-percent exchange rate between them, which the paper calls an elasticity. It also gives every design a "level" relative to the surface, log V, and a "position" along it.

The paper states that this is not the Pareto boundary. It is a soft support, fitted so that about 10 percent of designs lie beyond it. It is offered as an alternative to PCA for the question "what trades against what". It is also offered as a replacement for the weighted-criteria grid, though that wording is mine. The worked case is a cubesat model: reaction wheel, star tracker, panel span, battery, two controller gains and fleet size. It has 13 metrics and 3,000 randomly drawn designs per regime.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- Definition and proofs. The "invariant value" V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics with weights summing to one. It scales linearly if all metrics scale. The surface is V = 1. The one-term fit is a convex program, so any minimizer is global, but uniqueness is not claimed. A maximizer of V is weakly Pareto-efficient. The converse is not claimed.
- Several terms. The several-term fit is not convex. The paper offers a descent guarantee, a numerical stationarity check, and local optimality where the assignment margin is positive. A soft variant has a second-order certificate. Global convergence is explicitly open (Q1).
- Cubesat case, one term. One term gives a 7-way contention among unit cost, available power, pointing error, fleet cost, momentum headroom, settling time and depth of discharge. The exponents run from 0.04 to 0.26. The effective sample behind that fit is about 74 of 2,134 feasible designs.
- Cubesat case, two terms. Two terms split this into an 8-metric and a 7-metric contending set. Peak slew power leads a term of its own. The shares are 0.74 and 0.26. On term 2, unit cost against available power has an elasticity of about -0.9.
- Two regimes. Aggressive gains and stock gains give similar sets. Total mass enters only under stock gains.
- Decision table. From 2,134 feasible designs, six requirements and the near-frontier band leave 28. Table 2 reduces these to a few picks. A fine star tracker brings pointing error from about 0.003 to 0.0008 degree for roughly 0.56 M USD against a 6 M USD fleet limit. The paper says the method prices that question and does not answer it.
- Planted-answer check. On 20 synthetic tables with planted terms, the fit recovers all three terms in 16.
- Propose-predict-check. A regressor aimed 3 proposals at holes in the chart. They landed within the predicted band, but only by draining the battery to a depth of discharge of 0.39, which fails that requirement. The paper says this is two distinct designs and illustrates nothing about a rate.

## 3. What I would do next

I would take one real trade table from one of our programmes. Candidates are a bus configuration against mass, power, cost and pointing, or a qualification-test table. I would run the K = 1 fit and ask my own engineers whether the contending sets make physical sense.

Before that I would find out how sensitive the exponents are to the conditioning. The paper says rescaling one metric changes which term is tangent. I would also check how the answer moves if the designs are not a random draw. Ours are never random: they are a few hand-picked point designs plus whatever flew.

I would not use Table 2-style shortlists for a decision yet.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

"It is a log-space envelope fit that tells you which metrics fight each other on the edge of your data and at what exchange rate. It is honest about being a description of the sampled table and not a causal model. The cubesat example is a toy, and the paper says so. The cost model leaves out payload, comms and operations. The hardware numbers are illustrative. There is no schedule and the launch price is flat. The one concrete decision it frames, fine tracker or not, is a sensible one, and it correctly refuses to answer it. I would not trust it for our design until someone shows it on a table with real test data and real correlated noise."

## 5. What I believe the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

Method does not:
- Recover the Pareto boundary. It fits the supporting envelope of the sample, a soft version of it.
- Make causal statements. A hole in the chart is an opportunity only if the trade space is log-convex there.
- Handle non-log-convex trade spaces: dents, holes, a design-space region with a cliff.
- Handle discrete variables beyond treating them as continuous within a category.
- Choose K, the conditioning, or whether the hypotheses fit your table. Those are human validation.
- Account for schedule, risk, or anything outside the table's metrics.
- Say whether the fit holds for designs outside the table.

Paper does not claim:
- Novelty. It says "We claim no priority."
- Global optimality for the several-term fit.
- Uniqueness of minimizers.
- Statistical consistency (Q2), identifiability under biased data (Q3), or Pareto recovery (Q4).
- That PCA is replaced, or that successive tensions exist in a PCA-like sense.
- A convergence theorem for the design-space search of Section 7. That section is future work with no results.
- That the propose-predict-check proposals estimate a success rate.

As an engineer, "energy" in a spacecraft paper reads as battery energy. The cubesat has an "energy balance" metric too. The disclaimer arrives, but the word still crowds a real attribute.

## 7. Terms I had to guess

- "Posynomial": a sum of monomials with positive coefficients. The paper gives this at its first full definition; I knew the term from GP.
- "n-lemma": the set of n metrics that contend on a term. Guess: a list of metrics with non-zero weights, not a logical dilemma.
- "Contending set": the metrics with non-negligible exponents on a term, found at tau = infinity.
- "Elasticity": percent change of one metric per percent change of another along a term. My reading: constant slope on a log-log line.
- "Reach" (beta): the offset of the surface along a term's direction, the soft maximum of the scores.
- "Fit temperature" and "term temperature": softness of the maximum over designs, and over terms. My guess: tau is chosen so about 10 percent of designs lie above V = 1.
- "Energy differential": log V. Guess: signed distance above or below the surface in log units.
- "Near-frontier band": designs with |log V| < 1/tau.
- "Term share" and "cell": share of near-frontier designs whose largest term is that term, and that set of designs.
- "Participation threshold": 1e-3 exponent; below it a metric is drawn grey.
- "Sharp" versus "soft" weights: hard assignment of each design to one term, versus softmax weights.
- "Margin", "stable radius", "term gap": the margin is the smallest gap between best and second-best term for any design. The radius is how far the scores may move before an assignment flips. The term gap is a distance from KKT stationarity.
- "Balanced point": the point on the data's convex hull whose weights are the dual solution.
- "Holes" in the chart: regions of the (position, level) chart with no sampled design.
- "Pull-back": mapping fitted quantities back to the original attribute units.
- "MoE / MoP": measure of effectiveness and measure of performance, as in the usual systems engineering sense.
- "Conditioning": orienting, shifting and scaling attributes into positive larger-is-better metrics.
- "Stock regime" and "baseline regime": the same thing, the stock-gain dataset.
- "Standard scaler": unit log-spread scaling.

## 8. Questions that remain

- How stable are the contending sets under a different but defensible scaling of metrics, for example scaling by requirement threshold instead of log-spread?
- What does the method give on a table that is not a random draw, such as a design-of-experiments grid or a handful of point designs plus flight data?
- How many designs does it take? The one-term fit leans on about 74 effective designs here. What is the threshold below which exponents are meaningless?
- Is the cubesat set of 13 metrics a realistic one, with its closely duplicated metrics, and does the method's output change if duplicates are removed first?
- What are the real requirement thresholds the case would use, and does the 28-design shortlist change if they differ from the data-derived ones?
- What happens with a metric that is strongly non-log-convex, for instance a step in cost at a launch-vehicle class boundary?
- Where does the fleet size enter, and why is a fleet of 25 in the shortlist table?
- How does a level difference of 0.01 to 0.02 translate into a programme quantity such as mass, cost or margin?
- What is the run time and scale for, say, 100,000 designs and 30 metrics ("scalability and speed" is listed as future work)?
- What is the comparison against simply plotting the 2D Pareto fronts of the three or four metrics the executives care about?
