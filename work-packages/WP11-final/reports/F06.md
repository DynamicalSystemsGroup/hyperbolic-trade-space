## 1. What this is about

A method for reading a table of evaluated designs (rows are designs, columns are conditioned "metrics", strictly positive and larger-is-better) as a one-sided boundary. It fits, from above, a log-linear surface. The weights are exponents on the simplex, a weighted geometric mean V of the metrics, scaled so V = 1 on the fitted surface. The "fit" is a soft maximum (log-mean-exp, temperature tau) of the weighted log-metrics, minimised over the simplex. With one term (K = 1) this is a convex program. With several terms it is a max (or soft-max) of K such log-linear pieces, fitted by alternation. Each piece's exponents are read as elasticities between the metrics that "contend" on it (the "n-lemma"). Each design gets a "level" log V (distance inside or beyond the surface) and a position coordinate (a log-ratio chart). The pitch is that this replaces weighted-score trade-study tables with something reproducible and defensible, and that it scales better than dominance counting. The demonstration is a simulated cubesat with 13 attributes and about 2,100 feasible designs.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- Definition-level: V is homogeneous of degree one in the metrics (Theorem 1). The K = 1 fit objective is convex in the exponents, so any minimiser is global, with uniqueness not claimed (Theorems 2 and 3). The chart is a bijection (Theorem 6), and the pull-back to raw attributes is exact under log-affine conditioning (Theorem 7).
- A maximiser of V over a set is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient if all exponents are positive (Theorem 9). The converse is explicitly not claimed.
- For K > 1 the objective is not convex (an instance is given). The alternation descends, reaches numerical stationarity, and is locally optimal where an "assignment margin" is positive. A second-order test certifies a soft fit. Global convergence is open (Q1).
- Numerical, on the cubesat: the nondominated set is 236 of 2,134 on four attributes and all 2,134 on all thirteen. K = 1 gives a "7-lemma" (unit cost, available power, pointing error, fleet cost, momentum headroom, settling time, depth of discharge). K = 2 splits it into an 8-metric term (74 % of near-frontier designs, led by peak slew power) and a 7-metric term (26 %, led by unit cost and available power). The solver declares both K = 2 and K = 3 "certified", but the margin is tiny (0.0004 against a band of 0.009). On twenty planted synthetic tables, 16 are recovered.
- A decision illustration: six requirements plus the frontier cut 2,134 designs to 28, and the method "prices" the choice between a fine star tracker and cost without making it.
- A tested comparison (Appendix H): on synthetic tables, this method tracks a planted upper boundary, while PCA and HoroPCA track spread.
- Stated non-claims: no novelty, no replacement of PCA or Pareto analysis, no causal reading of exponents, no claim that the fit holds outside the table.

## 3. What I would do next with it

I would take a production data set I know well, for example hospital or bank-branch inputs and outputs, with a known DEA and stochastic-frontier answer, and run it as an output-only problem. I would compare this fit's per-unit level log V against the DEA scores (CCR/BCC, the super-efficiency variant and a robust quantile or order-m frontier) and against a corrected-OLS or stochastic frontier on the same log table. The question is how far the rankings and the "who is on the edge" sets agree, and what the exponents add that the DEA multipliers (the dual weights) do not. I would also run the rescaling experiment the paper itself warns about (a different scaler, a different metric orientation) and see how much the K = 1 contending set moves. And I would check whether the fit's "10 % above the surface" convention is equivalent to an order-alpha quantile frontier.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

"It is a smoothed, log-linear, multi-facet outer envelope fitted to a table of attributes, with a quantile-like knob (tau) that lets about 10 % of units sit beyond the frontier. It reports global exponents per facet in place of DEA's per-unit weights, and it gives a coordinate chart. The mathematics for one facet is clean, convex and checked symbolically. The several-facet fit is a heuristic with local certificates. The paper is unusually honest about that. The case study is a simulator, not a production data set, and the one head-to-head test is against PCA and HoroPCA, not against any efficiency-measurement method. Whether it is more than a soft-max version of a multiplicative DEA / piecewise-Cobb-Douglas envelope is argued in prose and not tested."

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

Does not do:
- It does not estimate the Pareto boundary or the production possibility set. The fitted surface sits at the 90th-percentile-like level by construction (tau is set so that about 10 % of designs lie above V = 1), and the paper itself leaves open when the surface recovers the Pareto boundary and not the supporting envelope of the sample (Q4).
- It has no noise or inefficiency model in the fit. Noise appears only as a population ridge identity (L6, L20), a first-order result, with no consistency claim (Q2).
- No standard errors or confidence statements exist for the exponents. The text says exponents "describe the sample and are not estimates with a sampling error".
- It cannot represent a non-log-convex edge, so concave or S-shaped regions of a frontier are missed (the paper says so, as "dents and holes").
- It does not give a global fit for K > 1, and it has no rule for choosing K beyond a few diagnostics that "decide nothing alone".
- There is no returns-to-scale treatment, no input/output orientation, no model of what a design could attain.

The paper does not claim: novelty or priority; causal exchange rates; that its terms are clusters of designs; that tensions are orthogonal or that successive extraction works; that the lemma scripts are formal verification (they are "pencil-and-paper substitute"); that the surface describes designs outside the table; and that the "certified" status matters for a decision. Its "Three claims" paragraph separates optimiser, description and decision.

## 6. Where I was confused, stopped or found something imprecise, in order

Then, in the same abstract, "It is fitted to the outer edge of the data ... a soft support that a few designs may exceed, not a regression." I expected one stance, boundary or model of the cloud. What is stated is a boundary-like fit that is explicitly not the Pareto boundary and not the sample's supporting envelope. I could not tell, until Definition 1, which of the three objects "frontier" meant. The Definition 1 paragraph ("Three objects") is the first place it is pinned, and only in one sentence each.

2. Section 1, "Contribution. ... dominance stops shortlisting: its nondominated set nears the whole table. Of 2,134 feasible cubesat designs, the nondominated set holds 236 on 4 attributes ... and 2,134 on all 13." I stopped here. All designs nondominated on 13 attributes is what a random draw over 13 attributes, several of them (unit cost vs fleet cost vs fleet size) mechanically opposed, would give. It shows that dominance has no bite at this dimension for this table. It does not show that this method does better at shortlisting. The paper's own shortlist (28 designs) comes from requirements plus the near-frontier band, not from the fit alone. I expected a comparison of what shortlist each method gives.

3. Section 1 main results: "The fitted reference surface is where the invariant value ... equals one (Theorem 1). It is not the Pareto boundary". Theorem 1 is only homogeneity. I expected the sentence's claim (where V = 1) to rest on a result about the fitted surface, and it rests on the definition of the offset beta.

4. Table 1, DEA row: "Shares: A one-sided envelope of the data, a score per unit; Differs: This work fits global exponents per term, not per-unit weights". The DEA I know also produces a piecewise-linear envelope with facets whose supporting weights are the multipliers, and a facet-by-facet reading is standard (reference sets, facet-specific marginal rates, "marginal rates of substitution along a facet"). So "global named structure versus per-unit weights" is a real difference in what is reported, but the per-facet exponents here are the log-space analogue of a facet's multipliers. The paper's main-text DEA paragraph calls this a difference and does not say it is the same kind of object fitted by a soft instead of a hard envelope. Appendix G.1 does list "Multiplicative DEA" (Charnes, Cooper, Seiford and Stutz; Banker and Maindiratta), a piecewise-log-linear envelope by linear programming, "A piecewise log-linear fit to the data from above, each piece a product of powers". That is very close to the K > 1, tau = infinity case. It is absent from the main text's Table 1. At tau = infinity, with sharp weights, this method looks to me like a multiplicative-DEA envelope with the exponent vector constrained to the simplex and a fixed number of facets. The stated differences are the soft maximum with a temperature and band, the analyst-chosen K, and the chart and pull-back. They are real as engineering; I could not tell whether they are more than that.

5. Section 2, DEA paragraph: "Our boundary is a soft maximum with a temperature, not a boundary set by extreme units." Then the convention that about 10 % of designs lie above the surface. I expected a discussion of what that does to the "frontier" (it is a partial or quantile frontier in the order-m / order-alpha family, which is the standard answer to DEA's outlier sensitivity). Neither the main text nor G.1 mentions that literature, so I do not know whether the authors regard it as the same idea.

6. Section 3, "Assumptions ... A2: exponents lie on the simplex." The simplex constraint (weights sum to one, nonnegative) turns the surface into a constant-returns form in all metrics. I could not see what happens to a unit that scales one metric and not the others, because everything is larger-is-better and there is no input/output distinction. This is a deliberate move (the paper says "homogeneity by the simplex in place of inputs, outputs and a returns-to-scale choice"). The consequence, that returns to scale are assumed away, is stated in G.1 only, and then as a difference, not as a limitation.

7. Section 4: "The invariant value V ... stays the same along a trade-off, hence invariant." V is constant along a level curve of V by definition. I expected "invariant" to mean something stronger (invariance of the exponents under a transformation). Theorem 1 gives homogeneity (V scales with the metrics), which is a different property from the one the sentence names.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- "Hyperbolic" (title): resolved by Appendix A and the main text: the level curves of V are products of powers set equal to a constant, rectangular hyperbolas when exponents are equal. It is not negative-curvature geometry and not the Färe-Grosskopf-Lovell hyperbolic graph measure (G.1 says so). I guessed the first reading from the title, then HoroPCA in Section 2 reinforced the wrong reading until the explicit sentence in Section 4.
- "Energy differential" / "level": my guess, the log of V, a log-distance of a design from the surface (log of a radial efficiency score, in DEA terms, with the sign reversed). The paper confirms "negative inside, positive beyond"; I still read it as the log of a radial (Farrell-like) efficiency, and the paper says it is "not a Euclidean distance".
- "Reach" (beta): the soft-maximum of the scores along w, i.e. the offset of the supporting hyperplane. I read it as the support function value.
- "Slack" (as in "mean slack"): not the DEA slack (a residual input or output excess). It is the average amount by which the surface lies above a design. I took it as the fit's loss.
- "Near-frontier band": designs with |log V| < 1/tau. My reading: the set of designs within about one soft-max width of the surface.
- "Effective sample size": inverse sum of squares of the softmax weights on designs.
- "Contending set" / "n-lemma": the metrics with nonzero exponent at tau = infinity in a term's cell. My guess: the active-multiplier support of the facet.
- "Participation threshold" (10^-3), "numerically zero" (10^-12), and the exponent cut for support (10^-9): three thresholds; I took them as conventions for deciding "this metric is in".
- "Cell": the set of designs for which a term is the largest, a facet's region of the data.
- "Sharp / soft weights": hard versus softmax assignment of designs to terms in the alternation.
- "Conditioning" (stage 2): the analyst's scaling, orientation and shift of attributes into metrics, not the numerical conditioning of a matrix. Appendix D.12's `conditioning_` is the numerical sense, so the word is used twice with two meanings.
- "Certified" (solver status): a local-optimality and stationarity declaration, not a global one.
- "Supporting envelope" (the tau = infinity fit): the linear-programming envelope no design exceeds. In DEA terms, the hard envelope for this K.

## 9. Questions I am left with

1. At tau = infinity and with K facets, how does the fit differ from the multiplicative-DEA envelope of Charnes and co-authors or Banker and Maindiratta, beyond the simplex constraint and the chosen K? Is the simplex constraint what removes returns-to-scale freedom?
2. Is the "10 % above V = 1" rule a quantile (order-alpha) frontier, and are there results about what it estimates as N grows?
3. How much of the K = 1 contending set is determined by the scaler? D.5 suggests one metric in seven moves between two reasonable scalers. Is that the method's signal or the scaler's?
4. What would an outlier in the data do? In DEA a single extreme unit can set the envelope. Here the soft maximum limits one point's influence to its softmax weight, but at tau = infinity and in the contending-set definition it does not. The contending set is defined at the exact maximum, which is the outlier-sensitive end.
5. The effective sample at the fit temperature is about 74 designs and at tau = infinity under 4. Is there any uncertainty statement for the exponents, by bootstrap or otherwise?
6. Why do the metrics with near-identical columns (Spearman at least 0.97) drop out entirely from every contending set, and what does that say about the reading of an n-lemma as "these metrics trade off"?
7. The K = 2 split: how does it differ from fitting K = 1 separately to the two groups of designs found by a clustering? The paper says the terms "add a model" over clustering with per-cluster correlations, but the own-rows refit that would make that comparison is reported as ill-posed (G-6).
8. What is G-1's tau = 38.10 and the 0.092, 0.190, 0.261 fractions, given the main text's 41.7 and 0.098? Stale ledger row or a different run?
9. For the executive decision (Table 2): what would the shortlist and picks be without the fit, using the six requirements alone and the same rules? How many of the 28 designs are inside the K = 2 band by the fit and how many would not be?
10. Does "weakly Pareto-efficient maximiser" connect to anything the case study uses? The theorem is about maximisers of V over a set, and I could not find where in the cubesat reading it is invoked.
