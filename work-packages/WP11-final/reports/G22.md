## 1. What it is about

This is a paper about a way of analysing a table of designs after they have been evaluated. My side builds the evaluation: a configuration (which wheel class, which tracker grade, panel span, battery capacity, controller gains) goes in, and numbers come out (pointing error, total mass, available power, unit cost and so on). The paper takes thirteen such outcomes, turned into strictly positive larger-is-better "metrics", and fits a single smooth reference surface to them. The surface is written as a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled to equal one on the surface. The weights are exponents, and they read as elasticities: how many percent of metric j you give up per percent of metric i you gain, while staying on the surface.

For several pieces of surface the paper counts how many metrics are in contention at once and names the piece accordingly. One metric binding alone, a "dilemma" of two, a "trilemma" of three. It is illustrated on a cubesat (a small satellite) model coupling power, mass and attitude control, built in a systems-modelling language (the parts and requirements views in Figures 2 and 3 look like SysML v2). The recurring phrase is that the method is "not the Pareto boundary" and "does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis".

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- Each design gets an "invariant value" V. V is a weighted geometric mean of its metrics. It doubles when every metric doubles. V = 1 marks the fitted surface, and log V is how far above or below the surface a design sits (negative is inside).
- With one term the fit is a geometric program, so it is convex in the log variables. Any minimiser is global, and uniqueness is not claimed. The fitted exponents are elasticities, and the (position along surface, level) re-description of the metrics can be undone.
- A maximiser of V is at least weakly Pareto-efficient. It is fully efficient if every exponent is positive.
- With several terms (the sum of several monomials, i.e. a posynomial) the fit is not convex. The paper offers three levels of guarantee. Level 1: the iteration does not increase the objective. Level 2: a numerical measure of how close a term is to stationary (the "term gap"). Level 3: local optimality conditional on the assignments of designs to terms. There is also a stopping declaration, and a second-order certificate for the soft fit.
- The fit is not forced to put every design on the surface. It is tuned so that about a tenth of the designs lie above it (Figure 4, fraction above V = 1 meets target 0.1 at a "fit temperature" of 42). Some designs lie beyond it by construction.
- In the case, at K = 1 only eight of the thirteen metrics get a non-negligible exponent. Unit cost and available power are heaviest (Figure 4). At K = 2, the two terms contend over different subsets of metrics (Figure 7, Figure 8), which one term would blend.
- Noise on the log-metrics acts like a ridge penalty and shifts the exponents in a predictable direction (Corollary 1), with the explicit caveat that the rates may still be wrong.
- Several things are stated as open, not established: four questions Q1 to Q4 and whether the search for terms is monotone.

## 3. What I would do next

I would not touch the fitting side. I would look at what my evaluation chain delivers and check whether it can give a table of designs with all outcomes defined and positive. In the figures I would find which of the thirteen metrics come from the configuration alone, which from a simulation or measurement, and which from a downstream analysis. Figure 3 shows exactly this split, and I would draw the same map for my own system. I would then try a small trial: sample a few hundred configurations, produce the table, and give the table to someone who can run the method. I would not attempt to understand the K > 1 guarantees before the one-term fit has been tried on my data.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

"It is a way to summarise a big table of evaluated designs as a smooth surface with elasticities on it, so you can say which outcomes really trade against which. It sits on top of whatever produces the outcomes, whether a simulation, a model or a test rig. It is not an optimiser and it is not Pareto analysis, and the paper says so itself. What you must supply is a large sampled table of positive, larger-is-better numbers. I have only skimmed headings, boxes and figures, so I do not know how much work the 'conditioning' step is."

## 5. What I believe the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

I believe the method does not do the following:
- It does not produce or improve the evaluation model. The conditioning of attributes into metrics is called the analyst's job elsewhere in this material; in the boxed statements, Theorem 7 (pull-back to attributes) says the conditioning "is invertible" only under the assumption stated there. I take it that I own the conditioning.
- It does not find the Pareto boundary. A fitted surface with some designs beyond it is a different object (Definition 1 names three).
- It does not tell schedule, risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption).
- It does not guarantee a global fit with several terms. Only the one-term fit has global guarantees.

The paper itself does not claim:
- Novelty ("The method claims nothing as novel").
- That the fit minimiser is unique.
- That the position-along-surface chart is part of the model. Figure 6 says the position is "a reading of the sampled designs and not part of the model".
- Convergence or stationarity of the multi-term iteration beyond what Theorem 11 says.
- That the noise result tells you whether the rates are right (Corollary 1).

## 7. Terms I had to guess

- "metric": a conditioned attribute, positive and larger-is-better. My guess comes from the figure axes ("conditioned unit cost (inverse USD)").
- "conditioned": transformed so it is positive and larger-is-better (inverting costs, shifting, taking powers).
- "invariant value V": a scale-equivariant score of a design; V = 1 on the surface.
- "fitted reference surface": the locus V = 1 at the chosen finite temperature.
- "fit temperature tau": softness of the maximum in the fit; large means closer to a hard maximum. It is chosen so that about 10 percent of designs exceed the surface.
- "term temperature kappa": softness of the sum over terms.
- "reach beta": an offset, how far the data extend along an exponent direction.
- "term / K": one monomial piece; K is the number of pieces.
- "posynomial": a sum of monomials with positive coefficients (from geometric programming).
- "n-lemma" / "contending set": the set of metrics with non-zero exponent in a term's exact-maximum fit.
- "binds": probably "constrains the term's value", for a corner.
- "participation threshold" (0.001): below it an exponent is treated as zero.
- "near-frontier": designs with |log V| less than 1/tau.
- "cell": the designs for which a given term is the largest.
- "chart" / "position along the surface": coordinates of log-metrics with overall scale removed.
- "energy differential": log V, a signed distance to the surface; I could not find the intended meaning of "energy".
- "regime": a scenario of the cubesat case (primary = aggressive controller gains).
- "MoP / MoE": measure of performance / measure of effectiveness.
- "weakly Pareto-efficient": no design is better in every metric at once.
- "support": the set of coordinates where the exponent is positive.
- "slack" and "mean slack F": the fit objective averaged over designs, probably the gap between the surface and the designs.
- "stopping declaration": a statement made when the iteration ends about what has and has not been verified.
- "elasticity": percent change of one metric per percent change of another along a term.
- "primary regime", "executives' measures", "the rules": unknown beyond the guess given.

## 8. Questions that remain

1. What exactly must my evaluation deliver: a table of how many designs, with how many metrics, and drawn how (random, space-filling, designed)? The effective sample size of 74 in Figure 4 makes me worry about how many samples are needed.
2. What does an exponent of zero for a metric mean for a metric I care about, and what should I do about it?
3. How does the analyst turn raw attributes into positive larger-is-better metrics, and which part of that is mine? Theorem 7 hints at the power-law form.
4. What happens when my outcomes are noisy or come from a physical test rig, with repeated measurements? Corollary 1 covers a Gaussian first-order case only.
5. What happens when some metrics are strongly collinear (power margin, energy balance, available power at 0.97 to 1.00 association)? Is the method's answer then unique or stable?
6. Which K should be used, who decides, and what is the cost of K = 2 compared with K = 1?
7. What are the four open questions Q1 to Q4?
8. How is "unit cost needs the largest fleet" in Table 2 connected to the fit, if at all?
9. What is in Section 11 "your own case": what do I run, in what language, and what do I hand over?
10. Is the method meant to compare two designs (a decision tool) or only to describe the sampled set? "log V ... not a ranking" suggests the latter, yet "the designs the rules pick" suggests selection.

## 9. Whether I would proceed to the whole paper

Yes, but to Section 11 and the case construction first, not the whole of the paper. The skim told me the method sits downstream of my work and does not ask me for anything exotic: a table of positive metrics. It also told me that the paper is careful about what it does not claim, which I value. What I still cannot see is the hand-over: the definition of the table, the sample size needed, how conditioning is chosen, and what happens to metrics with zero exponents. Those decide whether I connect my models. The multi-term theory I would leave until a one-term fit on my own data had been tried, since it looks aimed at the person who runs the fit rather than at the person who builds the evaluation.
