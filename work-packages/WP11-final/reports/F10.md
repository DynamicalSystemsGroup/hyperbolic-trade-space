## 1. What this is about

The paper, "Scaling Decision Analysis with Hyperbolic Trade Space Identification", proposes a method (HTS) that takes a table of designs, each with measured attributes, conditions those attributes into strictly positive "metrics" where larger is better, and fits a posynomial "invariant form" to the table. The fit gives a reference surface, a position and level for every design relative to that surface, and "n-lemmas": the set of metrics that trade against each other in one term of the fit, with exponents as elasticities. The method is an unsupervised, table-in and coordinates-out analysis, meant as a scikit-learn estimator and as a reproducible, inspectable trade study. The worked example is a synthetic cubesat model (power, mass, attitude control) with 3,000 sampled designs per regime, 2,134 feasible in the primary regime, and 13 metrics, read for two stakeholders (operator: pointing error; sponsor: unit cost and fleet cost). The paper says the evaluation is a black box to the method, so a simulator, a test rig or a human assessment would feed the same table.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- One term (K = 1): the fitted surface is a level set of a weighted geometric mean of the metrics. The fit is a convex program in the exponents at finite temperature, and a maximiser of this quantity over the table or any set of designs is weakly Pareto-efficient (Theorem 9). The converse is not claimed.
- Several terms (K > 1): the fit is not convex in the terms (Theorem 10). Three levels of guarantee are offered: descent of the objective (Theorem 11), numerical stationarity through a term gap (Lemma 1), and local optimality with the cells held fixed (Proposition 3, Corollary 2). A second-order certificate (Theorem 12) applies only to a soft fit.
- Cubesat, K = 1: a seven-metric contending set (unit cost, available power, pointing error, fleet cost, momentum headroom, settling time, depth of discharge), with total mass at 0.02 at the fit temperature but outside the exact-maximum support.
- Cubesat, K = 2 (chosen by the paper's checks, Section 9 and D.15): two terms, with contending sets of 8 and 7 metrics and shares 0.74 and 0.26. The paper reads term 2 as unit cost against available power with elasticity about -0.9 on the conditioned metrics.
- Recovery of planted terms: 16 of 20 synthetic tables recovered by the full fit (D.14); the four failures are listed with their thinnest-term row counts, and one failure is "certified" yet wrong.
- Decision table (Table 2): the 28-design shortlist and five rules give five candidate configurations. The paper states that the method prices the question and does not answer it.
- HoroPCA comparison (Appendix H): on synthetic tables, the method recovers a planted edge where PCA and HoroPCA track spread, with the hypotheses H1 to H4 stated with refutation conditions. The paper says these are controlled constructions and are not a benchmark.

## 3. What I would do next with it

- Take a table from a rig or a model of my own and run the fit at two or three temperatures and at K = 1 and 2, to see whether the contending sets hold (the paper's own D.5 suggests they will for halving or doubling the target fraction, but not for a different scaler).
- Check the exponents under resampling of rows (the paper says this is not assessed at K = 1) with a bootstrap, since the paper's stability tests perturb design variables at 1e-12, not the sample.
- Look at the conditioning decision first: rescale one metric and watch which term is tangent. The paper itself says a stable direction "answers that question and is not a context-free discovery".
- Use the chart and the level of each design to find which designs sit beyond the surface, and test those by building them, since the paper treats beyond-surface points as hypotheses only.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

It is a convex-geometry fit with one clear use: when the metrics trade off, it names which metrics trade on a given piece and at what elasticity, and it places each design on that piece with a level. It is not a Pareto front, it is not causal, and its K = 2 choice is a judgment. Read the main text's Section 4, Section 9 and the Discussion; the appendices are where the checks sit.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It does not give a global optimum for several terms, and the convergence of the alternation is open (Q1). The certificates are local, and the paper says "certified" is a status set by tolerances at the returned point.
- It does not estimate a sampling error for exponents. The paper says designed samples are not independent observations and the exponents "describe the sample".
- It does not show that the fitted surface is the Pareto boundary (Q4), only that some sampled designs approach it.
- It does not say how conditioning, the choice of K, or the 10 percent convention for the fit temperature should be made. The paper calls these the analyst's job and says they change the answer.
- It does not claim priority over Pareto methods, DEA, PCA, HoroPCA or geometric programming, and it says it does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis.
- It does not say that the fit holds for designs outside the table, or that a decision follows from the fit. The paper says a third validation is needed for that.
- It does not establish the cubesat numbers, which the paper calls illustrative. Launch is an unpriced slot, the cost is a lower bound, and the requirement thresholds are chosen from a ladder of candidates.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- n-lemma: a term's contending set, named by its size, and drawn as a face of the simplex of exponents (Figure 1).
- Energy differential (log V): a design's level relative to the fitted surface; positive beyond it.
- Reach (β_k): the soft maximum of a term's scores over all designs; the offset that places the surface.
- Fit temperature (τ) and term temperature (κ): how hard the design maximum and the term maximum are. ∞ means exact.
- Band (1/τ): the near-frontier range |log V| < 1/τ, within which a design's weight is within a factor e of a design on the surface.
- Margin and stable radius: the gap between a design's best and second-best term score, and how far the fit's parameters can move before a design's term changes.
- Share: the fraction of near-frontier designs in a term's cell.
- Participation and contending set: exponents above 1e-3 at the fit temperature, and the exact-maximum support above 1e-9.
- Elasticity: the ratio of two exponents, the percent change of one metric per percent of another along a term.
- Scaler and log-spread: a per-metric rescaling that equalises the spread of log-metrics so one τ serves all.
- MoE and MoP: measure of effectiveness (read by the stakeholder) and measure of performance (read off subsystems), per the Figure 3 caption.
- Tension: the paper's word for a term that trades a set of metrics.
- Propose-predict-check and kernel direction: a regressor from configuration to chart coordinates, and a future extension.

## 9. The questions I am left with

- Why is the 10 percent convention the right anchor for the surface, when the fraction above V = 1 moves from 0.10 to 0.30 for K = 2 at the K = 1 temperature (G-1)?
- What happens to the exponents when the rows are resampled, not perturbed? The paper says this is not assessed at K = 1.
- Why is K = 2 chosen when the resampling-movement check is larger at K = 2 than at K = 3, and K = 3 is "certified" too?
- How should a reader read "certified" alongside the failure at seed 8, which is certified and wrong by 0.20?
- Does the shortlist change with the near-frontier band chosen for the K = 2 fit (the paper uses the K = 1 band for both)?
- How many of the 28 shortlisted designs survive if the cost requirement is read as a bound on the per-satellite unit cost rather than the fleet cost?
- Is the "cheapest fleet" in Section 9 cheapest among the shortlist, and if so does the sentence need to say so?
- Does the stable direction of the metrics survive a different conditioning, such as the robust scaler, which changes the contending set (D.5)?
