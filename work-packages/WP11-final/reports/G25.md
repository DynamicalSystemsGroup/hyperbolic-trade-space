## 1. What it is about

A method for turning a trade-study table into something an executive can follow. Each design is a configuration; its evaluation gives attributes; after conditioning these become positive "metrics", larger being better. The paper fits a posynomial "reference surface" to the outer edge of the table (a soft support a few designs may exceed), not a regression and not the Pareto boundary. Each design then gets a position along the surface and a level relative to it (the "energy differential"). Each monomial term of the fit names the metrics that contend on it (an "n-lemma") and gives their elasticities. The method is set against Pareto methods, data envelopment analysis, PCA and HoroPCA, and is demonstrated on a cubesat model (power, mass, attitude control) with 13 attributes and 2,134 feasible designs in a primary regime plus a baseline regime. There is also a preliminary section on choosing where to sample next, which the paper says has no results.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- The invariant value V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics. The surface is where V = 1, and log V is a linear function of the log-metrics minus an offset. The chart (position plus level) is a reversible rewriting, and under log-affine conditioning the map back to attribute units is exact.
- For one term, the fit is a convex problem in the weights (a geometric program at finite temperature, a linear program at infinite temperature). Its exponents are read as elasticities. A maximizer of V is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient when the active exponents are all positive.
- For several terms the objective is not convex. The paper gives three levels of guarantee for the sharp alternation (descent, numerical stationarity through a term gap, and local optimality where an assignment margin is positive), plus a second-order certificate for the soft fit.
- Case: one term fitted at K = 1 gives a seven-metric tension (unit cost, available power, pointing error, fleet cost, momentum headroom, settling time, depth of discharge). At K = 2 it splits into two terms; the first is led by peak slew power, and in the second unit cost trades against available power with an elasticity of about -0.9.
- Decision: 28 designs pass all six requirements and sit near the frontier. The paper does not choose among them; it prices one open question, whether a fine star tracker (about 0.56 M USD of a 6 M USD fleet budget) is worth it.
- Robustness: under relative perturbations of 1e-12 the contending sets and the certificate statuses do not change; several printed numbers do move (see point 6 below).

## 3. What I would do with it next

Before relying on a reading, I would take one real table with known outcomes and check whether the n-lemma names and elasticities survive a second conditioning and a different target fraction. I would want the K = 2 and K = 3 contending sets reconciled with the figures (below). I would want the requirement set and the cost basis of the decision table written as one list, with the caveats that sit only in the appendix carried into the decision section. I would put the hyperbolic-space comparison (HoroPCA) in the same frame as the cubesat result, because they answer different questions and the reader needs to see both limits.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

It is a careful way to read a trade table as a geometric surface with named trade-offs, and the proofs and the chart are the strong part. It is not yet a decision tool: the reference surface is placed by a 10 percent convention, the contending-set naming has at least two thresholds and two readings, and the cubesat economics are illustrative. Read the figures against the text before quoting any of them.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

Per the paper: it claims no priority; it does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis; it does not establish global convergence of the alternation (Q1), statistical consistency under noise (Q2), identifiability of the active metrics under biased finite samples (Q3), or that the surface recovers the Pareto boundary rather than the sample's envelope (Q4); it does not estimate sampling rates; exponents describe the sampled designs and are not causal; interpretation and the choice of K are human validation.

What I believe it does not do: it says nothing about designs outside the table without a separate validation; the surface's height is set by the 10 percent convention rather than only by the data; the "hyperbolic" of the title only refers to the equal-weight two-metric case; the schedule, the risk, the payload and the launch slot are outside the model; and the momentum check is one slew, so a feasibility verdict on the small wheel is a one-slew reading.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess, with my guess

- n-lemma: a named set of metrics that contend on one term; the name borrows from dilemma and trilemma (two or three metrics). Guess: the number of contending metrics.
- Corner, edge, interior (Figure 1): one metric binds alone, two metrics trade, three or more trade. Guess: how many metrics have nonzero weight.
- Contending set: the exponents above a tiny threshold of the exact-maximum fit to a term's cell. Guess: the support of that fit.
- Participation threshold, numerically zero: cut-offs below which an exponent is treated as absent. Guess: one for naming, one for reading exponents at the fit temperature.
- Fit temperature (tau) and term temperature (kappa): how hard the soft maximum is over designs and over terms. Guess: larger means closer to the exact maximum.
- Band, near-frontier band: designs whose absolute log V is below 1/tau. Guess: a tolerance on how far a design is from the surface.
- Energy differential, level: log V. Guess: distance from the surface on the log scale.
- Reach (beta_k) and share: the soft maximum of a term's scores along its direction, and the fraction of near-frontier designs in a term's cell.
- Cell: the designs whose largest term is a given term.
- Effective sample size: inverse sum of squared support weights. Guess: how many designs the soft maximum treats as if equal.
- Elasticity: the percent change of one metric per percent change of another along a term. Given in the text; I took the sign convention as the usual one.
- Favours (ternary vertices in Figure 8): undefined; guess: the vertex where that metric's share is largest.
- MoE and MoP: measure of effectiveness (read by the stakeholder) and measure of performance (read from subsystems). Given in the text.
- One axis: two metrics so nearly the same that one stands for both. Guess: a rank correlation near 1.
- Stable radius, assignment margin, term gap, mean slack: quantities in the several-term guarantees. Guess: respectively, how far the terms can move before designs change term; the gap between a design's best and second-best term score; a non-negative measure of distance from the stationary point; and the average shortfall of the surface above designs.
- Certified, stationary, stationary without margin: the labels of the solver's stop. Guess: certified is the highest level; stationary without margin is a stop without a positive margin.
- Scaler (in the conditioning paragraph): probably "scaling". Guess only.
- Frontier: the paper distinguishes the fitted surface, the supporting envelope and the Pareto boundary; I took the word as needing the context each time.
- Proposal, hole, dent, novelty: a predicted sampling location; a region with no designs inside the envelope; a local concavity of the envelope; V minus 1 as relative improvement.
- Log-convex envelope, generalized geometric program (GGP), max-affine fitting, kernel direction: standard in geometric programming; I took them as used.
- Hypothesis and status codes (A, R, F, I, L-numbers, H1 to H4): labels defined in the appendices.
- Gap G-9, G-10, G-17 and similar: ledger entries I did not read.
- In the HoroPCA appendix, rho, t, R and "ball": a spread ratio, an interpolation fraction, a radius and the disk model. Guess from context.
