## 1. What it is about

A way to summarise a set of designs. Each design has several metrics, all positive and larger-is-better after conditioning. The method fits a reference surface through the designs: the level set V = 1 of a weighted geometric mean of the metrics (one term), or of a posynomial built from several such terms (K > 1). Each design then gets a level, log V, measured against that surface. The exponents on the surface are read as elasticities, the paper gives a chart of position along the surface plus level that is a reversible rewriting of the metrics, and each term gets an "n-lemma" naming the metrics that contend for it. It is demonstrated on a cubesat model that couples power, mass and attitude control, with thirteen traded metrics. The paper places itself beside PCA and Pareto analysis and claims no novelty.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- The surface is where w.u = beta, with u the log-metrics and w on the simplex. V is homogeneous of degree one, so doubling every metric doubles V (Theorem 1).
- With one term, the fit is convex in the log variables (Theorem 3), so every minimiser is global. Uniqueness is not claimed. My reading is that the fit minimises the soft maximum of the design scores, which sets the reach beta (see point 6.8 and section 7).
- On the surface, the elasticity of metric j against metric i is -w_i/w_j (Theorem 5). A design below the surface reaches it either by improving one metric j by V^(-1/w_j), or every metric by 1/V.
- The chart (Z, log V), with Z = Bu free of overall scale, is a bijection with the metrics (Theorem 6).
- Attributes map to metrics by a power law, so conditioning is invertible and a negative exponent marks a cost attribute (Theorem 7).
- For K > 1, V_K is the 1/kappa power of a posynomial. As kappa goes to infinity it becomes a maximum over terms (Theorem 8). A maximiser of V over any set of designs is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient when every w_j is positive (Theorem 9).
- The several-term fit is not convex in the terms. Its guarantees are: a descent statement, under a non-emptiness condition, that the mean slack does not rise, with a finite stop under unique minimisers and a fixed tie rule (Theorem 11); a numerical stationarity gap, which is zero exactly at the simplex KKT conditions (Lemma 1); a stable-assignment radius (Lemma 2); and conditional local optimality (Proposition 3). A soft fit has a second-order sufficient certificate (Theorem 12).
- Gaussian noise on the log-metrics adds a ridge to the objective and shifts the expected minimiser by a stated first-order formula (Corollary 1). That is the shift of the expected minimiser, not of one fit.
- In the cubesat case, at K = 1 and fit temperature tau = 42, the exponents in Figure 4 are unit cost 0.26, available power 0.20, pointing error 0.16, fleet cost 0.15, momentum headroom 0.11, settling time 0.07, depth of discharge 0.04 and total mass 0.02. Control effort, power margin, energy balance, peak slew power and battery peak power ratio are at zero. The K = 2 fit splits the frontier into two contending regions (Figure 8).

I checked by hand that Theorem 4 (log-mean-exp against log-sum-exp), Theorem 5 (elasticities and improvement factors), Theorem 9, Lemma 1 and Proposition 2 follow from their stated forms. The exponents printed in Figure 7 sum to 1.00 in each term's panel, and Figure 5's slope of about -0.8 agrees with -0.20/0.26 read from Figure 4.

## 3. What I would do with it next

For my own study I would take the list of metrics and condition each to a positive, larger-is-better form with transforms I can defend. I would fit K = 1 and choose tau by the paper's convention: the fraction of designs above V = 1 close to 0.1. Then I would repeat the sweep over tau, as in Figure 4, to see how much the exponents move. I would read the exponents as trade weights and draw slices like Figure 5 for the review, with the elasticity written beside the line. Each option would get its level and its place in the chart. I would check for collinear metrics before trusting any exponent. I would move to K = 2 only if the one-term surface leaves large contending sets. When inputs shift, I would refit and use the stable radius of Lemma 2 to decide whether the assignment held. I would give a noise sensitivity from Corollary 1 only if I could estimate the noise covariance.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

The paper fits one weighted geometric mean of the metrics to a cloud of designs and calls the level set through the cloud "the reference surface". Each design gets a level above or below it, and the exponents are the trade weights, with a formula for the trade along the surface. It is convex for one term, and the paper is careful about what it proves beyond that. The level is not a ranking and the surface is not the Pareto boundary. The cubesat example shows that a single term blends contending sets that two terms separate. The numbers come from a simulation.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It is not the Pareto boundary, and it does not rank designs by level (Table 2 caption, Definition 1).
- It does not claim uniqueness of the fit, or convergence of the several-term algorithm beyond the stated descent and stop (Theorem 3, Theorem 11).
- It does not claim that the several-term stop is stationary (Theorem 11).
- The second-order certificate is local (Theorem 12).
- It does not say the schedule, the risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption).
- It does not say whether the noise rates are right (Corollary 1).
- It claims nothing as novel and does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis (Main results).
- The cubesat results are simulated (Figure 3 box: "simulation here; field data when fielded").

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- n-lemma: a name for the set of metrics that contend for a term, indexed by how many there are. A corner is one metric, an edge a pair, the interior three.
- dilemma and trilemma: a choice between two or three metrics that cannot all be improved together.
- binds alone: one metric alone is the only active one for a term.
- contending set and support: the metrics with a positive exponent in the exact-maximum fit to a cell.
- cell: the designs whose largest term is that term.
- fit temperature tau: how hard the soft maximum is in the fit. Larger means harder.
- term temperature kappa: how sharply terms meet.
- participation threshold: a cut below which an exponent counts as absent at the fit temperature. Figure 4 gives 0.001.
- reach beta: the soft maximum of design scores along a term's direction.
- J_tau: my guess at the soft-maximum objective of the fit, see 6.26.
- log V and energy differential: the offset of a design from the surface, read as a level. The word "energy" is unexplained and collides with the metric "energy balance".
- slack: my guess is how far designs lie beyond the surface.
- effective sample size: probably a weighted count of designs, as in Kish's formula. Unconfirmed.
- near-frontier band: designs with |log V| below 1/tau.
- association and tension: Pearson correlation of log-metrics, with negative meaning tension.
- conditioning: a transform from raw attributes to positive, larger-is-better metrics.
- elasticity: the ratio of percent changes along the surface, Definition 8.
- primary regime: a setting of controller gains, "aggressive" according to the Figure 4 caption.
- MoP and MoE: probably measure of performance and measure of effectiveness, from systems engineering. Unconfirmed.
- SlewVerification and ConfigurationVerification: the kind of verification a requirement is tied to. Unconfirmed.
- posynomial: a sum of monomials with positive coefficients.
