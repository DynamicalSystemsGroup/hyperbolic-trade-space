# G13
Head: c23cf60

## 1. What it is about

A way to take a set of sampled designs, each with a few performance and cost metrics (larger is better, all positive), and fit one surface through them that marks where a design's "invariant value" V equals 1. V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled so that it equals 1 on that fitted surface. Designs above the surface are beyond what the sample reached; designs below it are inside. The surface is built from a posynomial (a sum of K power-law terms), with K = 1 a single monomial fitted as a geometric program. The worked case is a cubesat model: a bus, two-to-three reaction wheel classes, star tracker grades, solar panels, a battery and a controller, with thirteen traded metrics (control effort, settling time, momentum headroom, pointing error, energy balance, power margin, battery peak power ratio, available power, depth of discharge, peak slew power, fleet cost, unit cost, total mass). The paper says it wants to name which groups of metrics actually trade against each other in the sample, and to show how a single-term fit blurs groups that a two-term fit separates.

## 2. The main results, as I now understand them

- V is a weighted geometric mean scaled to 1 on the surface. Scaling every metric by k scales V by k. Its exponents are elasticities: on a one-term surface, the percent change in one metric per percent change in another is minus the ratio of their exponents. A design below the surface can reach it by improving one metric alone by V to the power minus one over its exponent, or every metric together by 1/V.
- For one term the fit is a convex problem in the exponents, so any minimiser is global, though uniqueness is not claimed. A maximiser of V is at least weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient when all exponents are positive.
- For several terms the fit is not convex. The paper gives a descent that does not raise the objective, a numerical stationarity measure (the term gap), a stability radius for the assignment of designs to terms, and a local-optimality statement conditional on the cells. It does not claim convergence or stationarity of the stop. A soft fit has a second-order certificate.
- In the cubesat case (Figures 4 to 8): at fit temperature 42, the exponents put most weight on unit cost (0.26), available power (0.20), pointing error (0.16) and fleet cost (0.15); five metrics drop to zero. With two terms, one region trades pointing error against control effort and another trades total mass against pointing error (Figure 8A); the other triple gives settling time against control effort and depth of discharge alone (Figure 8B). The authors read the sample's spread along the surface as a trade-off reading, not a result.

## 3. What I would do with it next

I would not rely on the surface until I had checked three things against my own programme. First, the fit temperature: the paper picks it so that about 10 percent of designs sit above the surface, which makes the exponents depend on a target I would have to justify, and the effective sample size at that temperature is 74 out of several thousand designs. I would refit at a range of temperatures and see which exponents survive. Second, the near-collinear metrics: in the 8-metric term, power margin, energy balance and available power correlate at 0.97 to 0.99 (Figure 7). A fit that picks one of three near-duplicates is picking by noise unless the metrics really differ, so I would pick one metric per physical quantity before fitting. Third, the cost side: launch cost is derived from total mass and marked as not traded, but a real launch price is usually a step function of mass class or a rideshare tier, not a smooth power law. I would replace that link with the actual price schedule and see whether the fitted exponent on total mass still holds.

For my own work I would also want the parts list completed: the cubesat assembly (Figure 2) has no structure, harness, thermal, propulsion or link budget, so both mass and cost are only as complete as those parts. I would run the same method on a configuration set with a real margin policy before using any of its trade-offs in a review.

## 4. What I would say to a colleague

"It turns a cloud of sampled designs into a surface and gives you the local trade ratios, by reading exponents as elasticities. It is a good way to see which metrics compete in a region of the sample. It is not a Pareto front, it does not rank designs, and it is only as good as the sample and the temperature you fit at." I would add that the paper calls itself a demonstrator on a synthetic or toy case and says so in the figure captions, which I found honest, but the numbers in the case are not yet something I would take into a design decision.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

From the text itself: the surface is not the Pareto boundary (Definition 1); the method does not tell schedule, risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption); it does not rank designs (log V is a distance, not a score); it claims nothing as novel and does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis (main results); convergence and stationarity of the several-term stop are not claimed (Theorem 11); the noise-induced shift in exponents is the shift of the expected minimiser, not of any one fit, and it does not say whether the rates are right (Corollary 1). Four questions Q1 to Q4 are listed as open (Section 6), and the monotonicity of the search is open (Section 7). The search itself is labelled preliminary, so the sample, and hence the surface, is preliminary too.

## 6. Points of confusion, stops and imprecision, in the order I met them

- Figure 1, caption and panel: "a synthetic table with three planted terms". The planting is stated, which is fine, but the mapping from an exponent vector to a corner, edge or interior ("n-lemma") is only explained in the caption. I had to infer from the figure that a corner is one metric binding alone, not a statement about the data.
- Figure 2, caption: "Every name is read from the system model; so are the ranges, which the view does not draw (Appendix C.1)." I could not see any range, so I could not judge whether a tune range such as the controller gains Kp and Kd is physically sensible.
- Figure 3, caption and box: "The slew experiment returns ten of the thirteen". The caption names six (three from configuration, settling time from the measured slew, unit cost and fleet cost from an analysis each). The box says the measurement gives control effort "(passed on)" and settling time "(not returned)". As far as I could count the arrows, they run to about eleven metrics. I could not reconcile ten with those statements, and I could not tell which four unnamed metrics are returned.
- Figure 3, total mass: total mass is a traded metric, yet the caption says it is read "through the launch cost (yellow: derived, not traded)" and the arrow from the launch cost is dashed "computed from". Whether total mass is a traded metric or a derived one changes whether the launch requirement constrains the fit directly, and I could not settle that from the figure alone.
- Figure 3, the word "simulation" in the measurement box: "simulation here; field data when fielded". Fine as a flag, but I would want to know which parts of the case rest on that simulation.
- Figure 4, caption: "primary regime (aggressive controller gains)". I did not know what a regime is in this paper, or why the gains define it; the phrase is used in several figures without a definition in my material.
- Figure 4, the convention: the fit temperature is "the temperature at which the fraction is within 0.01 of the target (a convention, Section 5)". I read this as choosing the temperature by an outcome, which is unusual. I would want the argument before accepting it.
- Figure 4, the heat map: the x-axis labels are evenly spaced although the top panels are on a log scale. That is a presentation choice, and it makes the columns look equally spaced in temperature when they are not.
- Figure 5, caption: "the line is the surface to within 0.1 in log V". On the unit-cost axis, 0.1 in log V is about 0.4 in log unit cost (the unit-cost exponent is 0.26), which is a wider vertical spread than I expected. The caption is correct, but the axis hides the scale of the tolerance. The slope of the line, roughly minus 0.8 to minus 0.9, is consistent with the exponent ratio 0.20 over 0.26 that I could read off Figure 4, so I accept the figure, though I could not check the data.
- Figure 5, axis labels: "conditioned available power (W; larger is better)" and "conditioned unit cost (inverse USD)". "Conditioned" is used throughout and I could not see what the conditioning does.
- Definition 3 and Theorem 8: the formula is the sum (a log-sum-exp across terms), while "the package's mean" subtracts log K over kappa. The two differ by a constant (log K)/kappa. The text says this changes neither exponents nor minimisers, but the level log V, which Figure 6 and Table 2 report, does shift. I could not tell which form the reported levels use, and so could not tell where the surface V = 1 sits.
- Definition 4 and Theorem 4: the fit uses log-mean-exp and the paper is explicit that this is not log-sum-exp. Good, but kappa (the term temperature) never appears with a value in the figures I have, so I could not tell how sharply the terms meet in Figures 6 to 8.
- Theorem 2: the labels A2, A3, R1 and the lemma codes (L3.9 and so on) refer to hypotheses and appendices I do not have. "R1 is a statement about the exact-maximum geometry; it does not imply that the finite-tau minimizer is interior" is a sentence I read twice and still could not place.
- Theorem 3 and the Main results: "convex in the logarithmic variables". The theorem is stated in the exponent variable w; the main results say logarithmic variables. I took the second to mean the metrics' logs and not the exponents, but that is a guess.
- Theorem 11, "Level 1": "stopping declaration" is used in the main results without a definition in my material.
- Figure 6, caption and axis: "The surface is the flat line log V = 0 whatever the position". This holds by construction of the chart (Theorem 6 makes log V and position separate coordinates), so it is not a finding about the surface, and the figure cannot show curvature. I noticed that only after reading the theorem.
- Figure 6, axis: "to the right, better unit cost, total mass and peak slew power". The axis is a reading of the sampled designs, not part of the model, but the label gives a direction of better. That is a ranking along the surface, which sits oddly with the claim that log V is not a ranking.
- Figure 6 caption: "the first principal direction of the near-frontier chart ... (36% of it)". The 36 percent is the share of spread, and the loadings are in Appendix D.16, which I do not have.
- Figure 7, the labels "8-lemma" and "7-lemma": the title counts metrics ("8 metrics in the exact-maximum support") while Definition 6 says n_k counts the contending set, which I took to be metrics too. The n-lemma naming for n above 3 is never given a picture or a sentence in my material, so the names read as jargon to me.
- Figure 7, the exponents: term 1 has peak slew power at 0.43 and term 2 has unit cost at 0.25. These sum to one for each term, which is a useful check, and I did it. But the cell's correlation matrix is computed on near-frontier designs, and with a 0.99 correlation between three metrics I could not see which one the exponent is attached to.
- Figure 8, against Figure 7: Figure 8 says term 1 in triple A trades pointing error against control effort, but Figure 7 gives term 1 a control-effort exponent of 0.00. Figure 8 is therefore a separate fit on three metrics at a time, and the caption does not say so. I had to work that out from the numbers.
- Figure 8, labels: the star and diamond are "where its V peaks", but V is said to peak on the surface, and the caption does not say how a peak of V is located within a triple.
- Table 2: only the caption is in my material. "The cheapest unit needs the largest fleet" is stated as a finding, but I had no numbers to check it against.
- Table 2 caption: "The shortlist and the rules are in Appendix D.19." The rules that pick designs are not in my material.
- Definition 1 and the main results: the "supporting envelope" is "the tau = infinity fit, which none of the table's designs exceeds". That is a statement about the sample, not the system, and the paper keeps the Pareto boundary separate. I agree with the separation but would want to see how the sample's envelope is said to bound the attainable set, if it is.
- Figure 2 and Figure 3 together: the parts list has nine parts, and the metric list has thirteen. Several metrics (energy balance, power margin, battery peak power ratio) are functions of the same few parts. The count of traded metrics is therefore partly a choice of how to slice the same physics.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- invariant value V: the weighted geometric mean scaled to 1 on the surface (stated, but I guessed its use as a level).
- fitted reference surface: the set where V = 1 for the fit, not the Pareto front.
- fit temperature tau: how hard the soft maximum over designs is (near hard max at large tau).
- term temperature kappa: how sharply terms meet in the posynomial.
- soft maximum / log-mean-exp: a smoothed max over designs, normalised by the number of designs.
- near-frontier band: designs with absolute log V below 1/tau.
- n-lemma and its named sizes (dilemma, trilemma, 7-lemma, 8-lemma): the contending set of a term, named by its size.
- contending set and support: the metrics with a non-negligible exponent in the exact-maximum fit to a cell.
- cell and term share: the designs whose largest term is this term, and their fraction of the near-frontier designs.
- reach beta_k: how far the data extend along a term's direction.
- elasticity: the ratio of exponents, read as percent change per percent change.
- ascent direction: the gradient of log V, i.e. the normal to a term's face.
- participation threshold: the exponent below which a metric is shown white (0.001).
- primary regime: a set of controller gains the case is run at; the paper's usage is unclear to me.
- conditioned metric: a metric after an analyst-chosen transformation; the transformation is not in my material.
- effective sample size: the number of designs the soft weighting counts as equivalent.
- stable radius: how far the term parameters can move before the assignment of designs to terms changes.
- ridge (in Corollary 1): a penalty on the exponents' squared size that noise adds to the fit.
- energy differential: the name the paper gives to log V.
- attainable set: all realisable designs, a property of the system.
- PCA first principal direction: the direction of most spread among near-frontier designs.

## 8. Questions that remain for me

- Which kappa and which level convention (sum or mean) produced the levels in Figures 6 and Table 2?
- How was the ten-of-thirteen split of the slew experiment decided, and which metrics does it return?
- What is the launch cost function, and does it depend on total mass in a way a fit can represent?
- What does "primary regime" fix in the controller and the mass budget, and what does the other regime look like?
- What is the conditioning applied to each metric, and does it cross the fit-temperature convention?
- How sensitive are the exponents to the choice among the three near-duplicate power metrics?
- Which hypotheses (A, I, R labels) are the load-bearing ones for the several-term results, and do any of them fail in the cubesat case?
- How are the categorical swaps (wheel class, tracker grade) turned into metrics the fit can use?

## 9. Whether I would proceed to the whole paper

Yes, but selectively. The material shows the authors are careful about what is claimed (the convergence and stationarity gaps are stated, the Pareto boundary is kept separate, the toy case is labelled), and the one-term results read as usable geometry. I would read Section 4 (definitions), Section 5 (the fit temperature convention and the appendix it points to), Section 9 (the case analysis), and the Appendix on the launch and unit cost model before I trusted any number from it. I would not rely on the several-term results until I had the hypotheses they depend on, because the labels I was given point to a lot of material I do not have. I would also want the parts list and the requirement set completed before I would use the case for anything beyond seeing how the method behaves.
