## 1. What this is about

A way to read a cloud of designs, each measured on several metrics that are all larger-is-better. The method fits one "reference surface" through the cloud. The surface is the set where a single number, the invariant value V, equals 1. V is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled so that it sits at 1 on the surface. The weights (exponents) add to one, and they say how the surface trades one metric against another. Each design gets a level, log V: above zero it lies beyond the surface, below zero inside it.

When one such surface blends two groups of designs that trade off different metrics, the method allows several "terms" (a posynomial) instead of one. The worked example is a cubesat model with thirteen metrics, fitted with one term and then with two. The paper says it claims nothing as new and sits beside PCA and Pareto analysis rather than replacing them.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- The surface V = 1 is a fitted reference. It is not the Pareto boundary, which is a property of the system; the fitted surface is read from the sampled designs.
- For one term, the fit is a convex problem in the exponents, so any minimiser is global. Uniqueness is not claimed.
- On the surface, the exponents give trade-off ratios: improving one metric by a percentage costs another by a fixed ratio of their exponents. A design below the surface reaches it by improving one metric alone by a factor V^(-1/w_j), or all metrics together by 1/V.
- The chart re-expresses each design as a position along the surface plus its level. The rewrite is reversible.
- With one term, a maximiser of V over a set of designs is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient if all exponents are positive.
- With several terms the fit is not convex. The paper gives a descent result (Level 1), a numerical stationarity measure (Level 2), a conditional local optimality result (Level 3), and a second-order certificate for the soft fit.
- In the cubesat case at one term, unit cost and available power carry most of the weight at the chosen fit temperature. At two terms, the designs split into regions that trade pointing error against control effort, settling time against control effort, and so on.

I spot-checked the statements that could be verified from the forms given: the degree-one homogeneity of V, the log-mean-exp bounds, the elasticity on the surface, the "within a factor e" claim for the near-frontier band, the term-gap bound in Lemma 1, and the ridge in Corollary 1. Each is consistent with the statement as written. I did not check any proof, since none were given.

## 3. What I would do next with it

I would read Section 9 first, to see whether the two-term fit really separates regions that the one-term fit blends. I would ask for the exponents to be re-estimated on resampled designs, because the effective sample size at the chosen temperature is about 74. I would also check how far the chosen fit temperature moves the answer, since the paper's own sweep shows some exponents thinning out near that temperature.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

It turns a cloud of designs into a few trade-off ratios and a distance-to-frontier number for each design. It is a rewrite of the data plus a fitted surface, not a model that predicts anything new. The surface is set so that about a tenth of designs lie beyond it, so it is a working frontier, not a bound. It is a sensible first look at trade-offs; it does not replace Pareto analysis or PCA.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

- It does not say anything about schedule, risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption, which says so explicitly).
- It does not locate the true Pareto boundary. The surface is placed by a convention that puts about 10 percent of designs above it.
- Its efficiency guarantee for one term is only weak when some exponents are zero.
- It does not establish the open questions Q1 to Q4, nor the monotonicity of the search (Section 7 is marked preliminary).
- It does not claim a global optimum for the several-term fit.
- It does not claim novelty.
- The "measured" slew in the cubesat case is a simulation, as the Figure 3 box says ("simulation here; field data when fielded").

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess

- Invariant value V: a weighted geometric mean scaled to 1 on the surface (stated).
- Reach beta: how far the data go in a given direction; I took it as the offset of the surface (stated in Definition 7).
- Participation threshold 0.001 and support threshold 1e-9: the cut-offs below which an exponent counts as zero.
- Contending set and n-lemma: the set of metrics that enter the exact-maximum fit for a term; its size names the term (Definition 6).
- Dilemma and trilemma: an n-lemma of two or three metrics.
- Energy differential: a guess, the level offset log V.
- Soft maximum: the log-mean-exp, with temperature tau; Definition 3's "mean" is this.
- Term share: the fraction of near-frontier designs in a term's cell.
- Cell: the designs whose largest term is that term.
- Slack F: the mean of the design-by-term gap, the thing the descent lowers.
- Effective sample size: the usual count of designs after weighting, 74 at the chosen temperature.
- Elasticity: percent change of one metric per percent change of another along the surface (Definition 8).
- "Shares", "fixed total", "where its V peaks": guesses, from the Figure 8 legend.
- Level 1, Level 2, Level 3, certificate: guarantee tiers, as the captions and theorems present them.
- "Conditioned" metric: a metric after the analyst's conditioning; the paper leaves this to the analyst.

## 8. The questions I am left with

- How stable are the exponents when the designs are resampled, given an effective sample size of 74?
- How much do the answers change if the 10 percent convention is moved to 5 or 20 percent?
- Which metrics are collinear, and how is the exponent split among them decided?
- Where do the six unexplained metrics on the Figure 3 experiment box come from?
- Which result is the "stopping declaration"?
- How much of the cubesat result depends on the simulation of the slew?
- Has the second-order certificate ever been checked on a numerical fit, and at what tolerance?
