Head: c23cf60

## 1. What this is about

A way to turn a table of designs, each with several metrics (all positive, larger is better), into a fitted "reference surface" in log-metric space. An invariant value V, a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, is scaled so that the surface is V = 1. With one term (K = 1) the exponents are read as elasticities. With several terms (K > 1) the frontier is split into regions, each led by one term (a posynomial-style soft maximum). Each design gets a position along the surface and a level log V. The running example is a cubesat model with 13 traded metrics and roughly two thousand sampled designs, in a "primary regime" of aggressive controller gains. The paper says the surface is not the Pareto boundary.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- V is homogeneous of degree one, and the surface V = 1 is where the invariant form equals one (Theorem 1).
- The K = 1 fit minimises a soft-maximum objective over exponents on the simplex. That objective is convex in the exponents, so every minimiser is global, though uniqueness is not claimed (Theorems 2 and 3, as far as I could infer the objective; see section 6).
- Exponents are elasticities, and the chart (position Z, level log V) is a reversible rewriting of the metrics (Theorems 5 and 6).
- A maximiser of V over a set of designs is weakly Pareto-efficient, and efficient when all exponents are positive (Theorem 9).
- If each metric is a power of an attribute with a shift, the fit pulls back to the attributes (Theorem 7).
- For K > 1 the fit is not convex in the terms. The paper gives a monotone descent (Theorem 11), a numerical stationarity gap (Lemma 1), a conditional local optimality result with the cells held fixed (Proposition 3), an approximate certificate (Corollary 2), and a second-order certificate for a soft fit at an exact KKT point (Theorem 12).
- Gaussian noise on the log-metrics adds a ridge and shifts the exponents to first order (Corollary 1).
- PCA on the near-frontier chart gives a reading of the trade-offs the sampling exercised, not a result (Appendix D.16).
- On the cubesat, the K = 1 fit has unit cost and available power as the heaviest exponents, and the K = 2 fit separates regions led by different term pairs (Figures 4, 5, 8).

## 3. What I would do next with it

- Build a model that gives positive, larger-is-better metrics for each design, and check that each metric is a power of an attribute with a shift (Theorem 7) before fitting.
- Run the K = 1 fit and sweep the fit temperature, as Figure 4 does, before reading any exponent. Check the effective sample size and how many designs sit in the near-frontier band.
- Use Theorem 5 to state trade-offs: the elasticity between two metrics on the surface, and the factor by which one metric must improve for a design below the surface to reach it.
- Check for near-collinear metrics before reading zero exponents as a property of the frontier (Figure 7 shows correlations of 0.97 to 0.99 among some metric pairs).
- Go to K = 2 only if the K = 1 surface visibly leaves a split in the designs.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

The method fits one smooth surface through the metrics of a set of designs, and reads its exponents as trade-off elasticities. The surface is a fitted reference, not the Pareto frontier, and its position depends on a fit temperature that the paper sets by a convention (the fraction of designs above the surface near 0.1). The single-term fit is a convex programme with a clean chart. The several-term fit has only partial guarantees, and the paper says so. The authors claim no novelty and do not replace PCA or Pareto analysis.

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

From the main results and the table and figure captions:

- It does not estimate the Pareto boundary. The surface is a reference, and some designs lie beyond it by construction.
- It does not rank designs. Table 2's caption says the level log V is "not a ranking".
- It does not give the schedule, the risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption).
- It does not claim uniqueness of the one-term minimiser, or stationarity and convergence of the several-term sharp alternation (Theorems 3 and 11).
- It does not establish Q1 to Q4 (section 6.3) or the monotonicity of the search (section 7). These are stated as open.
- The noise result does not say whether the rates are right, and it describes the shift of the expected noisy minimiser, not of one fit (Corollary 1).
- The Gaussian identity is a population identity with no consistency claim (Proposition 2).
- The second-order certificate needs first-order conditions to hold exactly, not within a tolerance (Theorem 12). A numerical fit does not meet that.

## 7. Terms whose meaning I had to guess, and my guesses

- "binds" (a metric binds alone): the term depends on that metric only, so its exponent is 1 at that corner.
- "energy differential" (for log V): a name for the log level, the offset from the surface.
- "n-lemma" and "dilemma" / "trilemma": a naming convention for the size of a contending set, with two metrics a dilemma and three a trilemma.
- "contending set": the metrics with non-negligible exponent in the exact-maximum fit to a cell.
- "participation threshold" (0.001): the exponent below which a metric is treated as not taking part.
- "u": the log-metric vector; "U": the matrix whose rows are the designs' log-metrics.
- "the package's mean": the log-mean-exp aggregator of Theorem 4.
- "mean slack F": the mean over designs of the slack from the surface.
- "fixed total" and "shares" (Figure 8): a normalisation so that the three shown metrics sum to one. I am not sure.
- "MoE" and "MoP": measure of effectiveness and measure of performance, a common systems-engineering pair. The figures do not say so.
- "reach" and "term share": the reach is the soft maximum of scores along a term's direction (Definition 7); the share is the fraction of near-frontier designs in a term's cell.
- "stopping declaration" and "Level 1/2/3": the paper's three levels of guarantee for the several-term fit, with a stopping rule I could not see.

## 8. The questions I am left with

- Which thirteen metrics does the slew experiment return, and which ten? (Figure 3 box and caption.)
- What is the objective J_tau, and what is U, exactly?
- How sensitive is the fitted surface to the 10 percent convention in Figure 4? What happens to the exponents under another target fraction?
- Do the zero exponents of power margin and energy balance (correlated about 0.99 with available power, Figure 7) reflect the frontier, or the choice among near-duplicate metrics?
- Does n_k stay the same under a different numerical tolerance than 10^-9?
- Is the PCA position of Figure 6 related to the chart Z of Definition 5, and if so how? Z depends on an arbitrary basis B.
- What are Q1 to Q4?
- What is the stopping declaration, and what does a user of the several-term fit actually receive at each level?
- How many designs does a fit need in practice? Figure 4 gives an effective sample size of 74 at the fit temperature, from about two thousand designs.
- What do Table 1 and Table 2 contain?

## 9. Would I read the whole paper, and why

Yes. The idea is clear enough to try on my own data, but the claims I would rely on rest on the methods section (the fit temperature convention, the thresholds, and the definition of the objective), on the result sections that qualify the several-term fit (6.2 and 6.3), and on the cubesat case analysis, where the figures are only partly explained by their captions. I would also want Table 2's rules, which the material did not include. I would read it looking for the points where a number depends on a convention, since several of the results I could see do.
