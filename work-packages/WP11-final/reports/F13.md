## 1. What this is about

A method for reading a table of candidate designs, each scored on many metrics, without choosing weights up front. Every metric is first conditioned to be positive and larger-is-better. A "fitted reference surface" is then drawn through the data. It is a weighted geometric mean of the metrics, scaled so that V = 1 on the surface and V doubles if every metric doubles. The weights are called exponents and double as elasticities, meaning how many percent of metric j you give up per percent of metric i along the surface. With several terms (a posynomial) the surface is piecewise. Each piece is labelled by how many metrics are in contention there (a corner, a "dilemma" of two, a "trilemma" of three or more). The demonstrator is a cubesat model (reaction wheel, star tracker, solar panel, battery, controller gains) with 13 traded metrics, swept in a design table of a few thousand designs. The metrics are things like pointing error, settling time, total mass, available power, depth of discharge, unit cost and fleet cost.

## 2. The main results, as I understand them

- The reference surface is not the Pareto boundary. It is a smooth-max fit to the cloud. Some designs sit above it (V > 1), by construction, about 10 percent at the chosen fit temperature. A separate envelope (tau = infinity) is the one nothing exceeds.
- With one term, the fit is a convex problem (a geometric program), so any minimiser is global, though uniqueness is not claimed. The exponents are elasticities. A maximiser of V is at least weakly Pareto-efficient.
- With several terms the fit is not convex. The paper gives three levels of guarantee: the objective does not rise per iteration, a numerical stationarity gap, and a local-optimality statement conditional on the cell assignment. A second-order certificate applies for the soft fit. There is a stopping declaration, but convergence in general is not claimed.
- The (position, level) chart is a reversible rewriting of the metrics. PCA on the near-frontier designs reads the trade-offs the sampling exercised, and the paper says this is a reading, not a result.
- On the cubesat, two terms separate contending sets that one term blends. One region trades pointing error against control effort and another trades total mass against pointing error. Another triple has settling time against control effort and depth of discharge alone.
- Four questions (Q1 to Q4) and the monotonicity of the search are left open.

## 3. What I would do next

I would take a table from one of my own trade studies (a launch vehicle stage or a bus), condition it, and fit K = 1 and K = 2 to see whether the contending sets match what my team already knows from the margin and sensitivity tables. I would check that the 13 metrics are not just restatements of a few (see below). I would also run it with a sweep that is not a uniform or random one, such as a real downselected design family, because the fit depends on where the samples are.

## 4. What I would tell a colleague

"It is a tool for reading a big trade table. It tells you which metrics fight each other near the frontier and by how much, and it shows that the fight changes by region. It does not find the best design or give you a Pareto front. Try it if you have thousands of runs and no idea which requirements are really in tension. Do not use it as a source of requirements or as a cost model."

## 5. What the method does not do, and what the paper does not claim

The method does not give the true Pareto boundary, rank designs, or tell schedule, risk, what to buy beyond the modelled parts, or what a larger fleet is worth (Table 2 caption says this). It does not prove the several-term fit is globally optimal. It does not claim novelty and does not replace PCA or Pareto analysis. It does not claim uniqueness of the one-term minimiser. It does not treat the PCA position axis as part of the model. Within what I was given, nothing is said about validation against flown hardware, or about a design family chosen by something other than sampling.

## 7. Terms I had to guess

- "Invariant value V": a scale-free score, geometric-mean-like, equal to 1 on the surface. I took it as a normalised figure of merit.
- "Reach beta": how far out the data go in a direction, so effectively the offset that places the surface at the data's outer edge.
- "Fit temperature tau": the sharpness of the soft max; low tau averages over many designs, high tau tracks only the best. A smoothing-versus-envelope knob.
- "Term temperature kappa": how sharply the pieces of a multi-term surface blend.
- "Contending set": the metrics with non-zero weight in a region, meaning those that actually limit improvement there.
- "Cell": the set of designs assigned to a term, like a Voronoi region.
- "Participation threshold" (0.001): below this a metric is treated as zero weight.
- "Energy differential": the level log V. I do not see why it is called energy.
- "Weakly Pareto-efficient": nothing is strictly better in every metric.
- "Effective sample size": the count of designs that carry weight in the fit.
- "MoP / MoE" in Figure 3: measure of performance and measure of effectiveness, as in systems engineering.
- "Conditioned": transformed so that it is positive and larger-is-better, such as inverse of cost.
- "Elasticity", as defined, is the local percent trade along the surface and is not the sensitivity of a metric to a design variable.

## 8. Questions I am left with

- Which of the 13 metrics are really independent? Several are correlated at 0.97 to 1.00. Does the method degrade, or do the lemma sizes inflate, when metrics are restatements of each other?
- How many designs are in the table, how were they sampled, and how stable are the exponents under resampling, given n_eff = 74 at the chosen tau?
- How is fleet size set, and why does unit cost correlate only -0.44 to -0.56 with fleet cost?
- Why is total mass so light (0.02) in a mass-driven domain, and does the result change if launch cost is traded instead of mass?
- What are the requirement thresholds, and how many of the designs meet all of them? A reference surface through the whole cloud says nothing about feasibility against requirements.
- How representative is a single slew experiment of the control metrics?
- What does the method give a programme that a sensitivity table or a Pareto plot of the top few metrics would not? The paper says it does not replace those.
- How does a design variable (wheel class, battery capacity) map to a move on the surface? The surface lives in metric space, and I make decisions in configuration space.
- What is the effect of measurement noise that is correlated across metrics?

## 9. Whether I would read the whole paper

Yes, but selectively. The honesty of the claims (every theorem narrows what it does not give, and the abstract-level statements disclaim novelty and Pareto replacement) makes me willing to look at the case itself. I would go straight to Sections 8 and 9 and Table 2, and to Appendix C.1 for the ranges and Appendix D.19 for the shortlist rules. I would read the theory sections only as far as needed to trust the exponents. What I have seen so far is a method with a clean mathematical core and a demonstrator that is plausible but thinly specified from a hardware point of view. The case is a toy in the way that matters to me (one slew, redundant power metrics, a fixed bus), and the paper says it is a demonstrator, not a design.
