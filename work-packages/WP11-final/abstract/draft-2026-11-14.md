A trade study must produce an answer and a justification an accountable decision-maker can follow. Machine learning
techniques can make principled scientific workflows scalable, reproducible and interpretable. For example, principal
component analysis (PCA) identifies basis vectors which explain the variance in a dataset. Our method, hyperbolic trade
space (HTS), explains trade-offs in a dataset of designs and measurements of their performance on real-valued
attributes. Trade-offs trace hyperbola-like frontiers: in order to get more of one objective, we must tolerate less of
another. When supplied with a dataset we first condition the attributes into metrics that are strictly positive and
"all else being equal, more is better." We do not assume the design space is well behaved, as it is often a mix of
categorical and numerical values, on different scales and with different units. While we apply conditioning to ensure
the solvers behave in practice, we do not make strong assumptions about the sampling distribution, only that the
frontier is log-convex. To discover trade-offs among many designs and many metrics, we
define the family of hyperbolic trade spaces by the level sets of a posynomial function of the metrics. The monomial
case is a convex optimization problem, and the posynomial case is compatible with generalized geometric programming.
Like PCA, HTS is an unsupervised learning method: PCA fits a subspace and projects the data onto it; HTS fits a surface
one dimension below the data and places each design by its position on it and its level above or below it, losing
nothing. By assuming this hyperbolic shape and solving for the "energy" level sets, the resulting fit
describes the trade-off space. Expanding on the concept of a dilemma or trilemma, a monomial term in n metrics
characterizes an n-lemma ("nye-LEM-uh"). Pareto improvements increase energy, and a design that maximizes energy is
weakly Pareto-efficient. White space within an occupied level set may be an under-explored region of the design space.
The paper provides a literature review, formal results and a worked example to demonstrate HTS's applicability to
decision analysis. Our proofs are scripted with SymPy and our implementation of HTS is compatible with scikit-learn.
The worked example is a cubesat design optimization problem with competing objectives in performance and cost. The
underlying design problem is characterized by a SysML v2 model, and a simulation study scores each design on thirteen
metrics. One term merges the subsystems' trade-offs; two terms separate them. High-dimensional n-lemmas are detected
and used to inform a design decision by stakeholders who care primarily about cost and performance, without losing the
thread back to the source designs. A point of the trade space pulls back to the design that produced it; a point
without data is a conjecture about a design, proposed by a rule and settled by building and measuring it. Future
work has two key thrusts: (1) searching the design space by sampling white space and pushing the frontier, and (2)
formal verification and performance by moving beyond Python into Rust and using theorem provers.
