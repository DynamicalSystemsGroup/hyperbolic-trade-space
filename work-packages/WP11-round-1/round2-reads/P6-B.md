Report on "Scaling Decision Analysis with Hyperbolic Trade Space Identification", from the figures, captions, headings and boxed statements supplied.

1. What this is about

A table of designs, each scored on d metrics that have been conditioned to be positive and larger-is-better (here d = 8 for a cubesat: settling time, control effort, pointing error, energy balance, power margin, depth of discharge, peak slew power, total mass, all as inverses or margins). The authors fit a parametric surface through the upper reaches of this cloud in log-metric space and read everything else off that surface. With one term (K = 1) the surface is a hyperplane w·u = β in log coordinates with w on the simplex, that is, a single Cobb-Douglas (monomial) facet; in the original metrics it is a hyperbola-shaped level set, hence "hyperbolic". With K terms it is the zero set of a log-sum-exp of K such affine pieces, that is, a softened maximum of K hyperplanes in log space, whose inside is an intersection of half-spaces with nonnegative normals: a free-disposal polyhedron in logs, with a concave piecewise-log-linear upper boundary. The value V(m) = Π m_j^{w_j} e^{-β} is homogeneous of degree one, so scaling every metric by 1/V brings a design onto the surface. In my vocabulary that is an equiproportional (Farrell/Shephard) output distance to a parametric reference technology, and the K-term object is a low-facet-count piecewise Cobb-Douglas envelopment with smoothed joins.

The fitting rule for one term is to choose w on the simplex to minimise the soft maximum (log-mean-exp at temperature τ) of the scores w·u_i over the data. At τ = ∞ that is min over w in the simplex of the largest score: the tightest supporting hyperplane with a simplex normal, an LP whose dual picks the mixture of designs that is best in its worst log-metric. At finite τ the offset β is a soft support function, so a fraction of designs sit beyond the surface; τ is set where that fraction first reaches 0.1 (Figure 4). The surface is therefore deliberately not an envelope but something closer to a partial or quantile-type frontier through the top tenth.

The application is engineering trade-space analysis from a SysML model (Figures 2 and 3): configurations are swaps of discrete parts and tunings of continuous parameters; the metrics are read from requirements; the paper is written for people who do not speak DEA.

2. The main result

As stated, there are several, and they are of unequal weight.

The mathematical core for K = 1 is that the fit is a convex program in log variables (Theorems 2 and 3: gradient is a softmax-weighted mean of the rows, Hessian is τ times a weighted variance), its exponents are the marginal rates of transformation along the facet (Theorem 5, Definition 8), and (Z, log V) is a reversible change of coordinates splitting a design into a position along the surface and a level above or below it (Theorem 6). Theorem 9 says a maximiser of V is weakly Pareto-efficient, efficient if all w_j > 0. For K > 1 the honest result is Theorem 11 plus Lemma 1, Lemma 2 and Proposition 3: the alternation (reassign each row to its maximising term, refit each term on its cell) does not raise the mean slack, a per-term duality-type gap certifies each refit, an assignment margin gives a radius inside which the partition is stable, and on that neighbourhood the returned point is a local minimiser with a computable gap to the fixed-partition optimum. Theorem 12 adds a second-order sufficient condition for the soft-weighted variant.

The empirical claim is in Section 9 and Figures 8 and 9: on the cubesat the K = 3 terms separate "contending sets" (the support of the facet normal) that the single K = 1 facet blends.

My reading of what is new and what is not, which the question asked of me:

- The one-term fit is a common-weights, output-only, log-linear frontier. Its convexity is the standard convexity of a monomial fit in log variables (geometric programming) and the max-min LP at τ = ∞ is textbook. Theorem 9 is the classical result that a positively weighted scalarisation (here a weighted sum in logs, that is, a geometric-mean or weighted-product aggregator) is maximised at a weakly efficient point. Theorem 4 is the log-mean-exp sandwich. These are correct and clearly stated, but they are not findings.
- The K-term surface is a softmax-affine (log-sum-exp of affine) function fitted by partition alternation. That class and that algorithm are known: max-affine and softmax-affine fitting in the convex-regression and GP-fitting literature, with the same Lloyd-type monotonicity that Theorem 11 states. The piecewise Cobb-Douglas envelopment itself is the multiplicative DEA model (Charnes, Cooper, Seiford and Stutz; Banker and Maindiratta's piecewise log-linear frontiers), which gives all facets implicitly; this paper fits a small chosen number of facets explicitly. The equiproportional distance V is the hyperbolic/radial measure of Färe, Grosskopf and Lovell restricted to outputs.
- The parts that I would call genuinely the paper's own, as far as this slice shows: (a) the temperature-controlled reference surface with a target exceedance fraction, which turns an envelope into a smooth partial frontier without a stochastic model; (b) the explicit reading of a facet normal's support as the set of metrics that actually trade on that facet, with the simplex-face picture of Figure 1 and the planted-term recovery; (c) the certificates (Lemma 1, Lemma 2, Proposition 3, Corollary 1, Theorem 12) packaged as three levels of guarantee and a stopping declaration for the non-convex K > 1 fit; (d) the discipline of saying, in every box, what is not claimed. The difference from what I know is partly real (b, c) and partly vocabulary (invariant value, energy differential, reach, n-lemma, contending set are new names for distance function, log-distance, support function, facet dimension, facet support).

3. What I would do next with it

- Run a multiplicative DEA on the same 8-metric cubesat table (output-only, free disposal in logs), enumerate the full-dimensional efficient facets, and compare their normals to the three K = 3 exponent vectors. If the three terms are recognisable clusters of DEA facets, the method is a facet-compression device and should be described as one; if not, I want to know what the soft maximum has done that the hull did not.
- Bootstrap or jackknife the fit. Figure 4 reports an effective sample size of 23 at the fit temperature from a cloud of several hundred: the K = 1 facet rests on about two dozen designs. I would resample and watch the contending set. In particular, energy balance and power margin are correlated at 0.96 to 0.99 in every cell shown (Figures 8 and 9), and the fit gives energy balance w = 0.18 and power margin w = 0.00 in the primary regime but power margin w = 0.18 and energy balance w = 0.02 in the stock regime. That is the familiar zero-multiplier problem with near-collinear outputs: which of the two is "contending" is not identified by the data, and I would want the paper to say so rather than let the n-lemma vocabulary suggest a finding.
- Vary the 0.1 target and watch what moves; this is the order-α question in a new coat, and the sensitivity of the surface and the supports to that choice is the first thing a referee in my field will ask.
- Fit the same data with the Aigner-Chu LP frontier (sum of slacks, all points on one side) and with convex nonparametric least squares in logs, and compare the elasticities. The cubesat is a case where the physics is known, so the fitted marginal rates of transformation can be checked against the model's actual local trade-offs rather than merely interpreted.
- Look hard at term 3 (Figure 6, bottom; Figure 8). Pointing error and peak slew power correlate at −1.00 in that cell because the designs sit on a lattice set by two categorical choices (tracker grade, wheel class). A facet spanned by categorical alternatives has elasticities that are artefacts of the catalogue, not of a trade-off; DEA has a literature on categorical variables for exactly this reason.
- Try it on a classical efficiency dataset with inputs and outputs to see whether the conditioning step ("inverse kg", "inverse s") survives contact with a real input-output structure.

4. What I would tell a colleague

It is a parsimonious parametric frontier in log-metric space: K Cobb-Douglas facets joined by a soft maximum, fitted by minimising a softened support function with the temperature chosen so about a tenth of the units lie beyond. V is a Shephard-type output distance to that surface, and the paper is candid that the surface is not the Pareto boundary (Definition 1 keeps three objects apart: fitted surface, τ = ∞ envelope, Pareto set). The one-term theory is standard GP and scalarisation material, correctly stated. The K > 1 theory is honest Lloyd-type monotonicity plus local certificates, with global optimality explicitly disclaimed. The new parts are the temperature as a partial-frontier knob, the reading of facet supports as "which metrics really trade here", and the chart coordinates. The application is engineering design from a systems model, where nobody uses our vocabulary, which is probably why it reads as new. The case study is well drawn and the figures explain themselves, but the contending-set story has an identifiability problem with collinear metrics that the authors' own correlation maps display. Read Section 2's Table 1 and Appendix G before forming a view; what matters is whether multiplicative DEA, the hyperbolic measure, Aigner-Chu and partial frontiers are in that table.

5. What I think the method does not do

- It does not estimate the Pareto boundary and does not claim to; Theorem 9 goes one way only.
- It has no statistical model: no noise term, no consistency, no confidence statement (Proposition 2 says "a population identity, with no consistency claim"). The temperature is a smoothing parameter, not an estimator of anything; the 0.1 target is a choice.
- It does not identify the facet support when metrics are collinear; the "n-lemma" of a term can swap members between two near-identical metrics, as Figures 8 and 9 show.
- It does not handle inputs and outputs as such; everything must be made positive and larger-is-better beforehand, and that conditioning (Theorem 7 shows it is invertible) is pushed to the analyst.
- It imposes homogeneity of degree one along the all-ones direction in logs, so the level sets are homothetic; that is a shape restriction, not a discovery.
- It gives one weight vector per facet, not a most-favourable weight per unit, so it is a common-weights measure within each cell; some will call that a virtue, but it means a unit's V is not its own best case.
- For K > 1 it does not find a global optimum, does not say in this slice how K is chosen, and Figure 8 shows a term (term 1) with three near-frontier designs, which is a facet resting on almost nothing.
- It does not tell the decision-maker what to choose; Section 7 (search) is marked preliminary.
- It requires strictly positive metrics (A1); zeros and the common "inverse of a quantity that can be zero" conditioning will need care.

6. Where I was confused or stopped

- Title: "Scaling Decision Analysis with Hyperbolic Trade Space Identification". I could not tell whether "Scaling" means scaling the analysis up or refers to the scale-invariance (homogeneity) that the paper calls the invariant form. "Hyperbolic" also collides with the hyperbolic graph efficiency measure in my literature; I assumed the collision is accidental.
- Main results: "The fitted reference surface is where the invariant value ... equals one; it is not the Pareto boundary but the level from which every design's position and level, and each term's elasticities, are read (Theorem 1)." The boxed Theorem 1 is only homogeneity and the log form; the gloss attributes to it a scope the box does not carry.
- Main results: "PCA and the fit are chained: the principal directions of the near-frontier chart read the trade-offs the sampling exercised, a reading and not a result (Appendix D.16)." Listed under main results and described as not a result in the same sentence.
- Main results: "Numerical: the status a fit reaches. Open: Q1 to Q4 and the search's monotonicity." Unintelligible without the section it points to.
- Theorem 2: "Balanced hard-support geometry does not imply an interior minimizer at finite τ." I do not know what balanced hard-support geometry is; I guessed it means a τ = ∞ solution whose supporting designs are symmetric enough that one might expect an interior w, and the sentence warns that the softmax can still push w to a face.
- Theorem 10 (i): "a convex program symmetric in the terms attains its minimum with all terms equal, so the objective over all terms is not convex in them in general." I stopped to reconstruct the argument: if the objective were convex and permutation-symmetric in the terms, the symmetrised point (all terms equal) would be a minimiser, which collapses K terms to one, so a useful K-term objective cannot be convex. Correct, but it is stated as a conclusion without the step.
- Theorem 11 hypotheses: "step B, unpenalized, with support offsets β_k = J_τ(Uw_k)". Step B is not defined in anything I was given; I took it to be the refit half of the alternation.
- Definition 3: "Term aggregation: sum by default, or mean; the formula is the sum." I could not see what the mean alternative changes beyond a shift of (log K)/κ in the offsets, and the sentence does not say.
- Definition 6 against Figure 4's caption: the contending set uses "exponents above 10^-9" at τ = ∞, while Figure 4 uses "an exponent at most 0.001 (the glossary's participation threshold)". Two thresholds, two temperatures; it took the outlined-but-grey cells of Figures 8 and 9 to see that a metric can be in the support at τ = ∞ and below participation at the fit temperature.
- Figure 4: "the effective sample size with its value at the fit temperature" (n_eff = 23). The figure reports it and the slice never returns to it. For me this is the most important number in the paper and it is left as an annotation.
- Figure 3 caption: "the analysis computes the other six, pointing error and total mass from the configuration alone." I read this three times; the box in the figure says "returns 7 of 8 metrics", and I eventually settled on: six computed by the analysis, of which pointing error and total mass need no slew, plus control effort passed on from the measurement, settling time taken from the measurement directly.
- Figure 8 title line: "Too few near-frontier designs for an association reading: 3 of ten". Three near-frontier designs in a term's cell is a finding about the fit, not only a reason to omit a heat map; I stopped here because Figure 6 draws term 1's segment through a dense cloud and I could not reconcile the two until I remembered the band is |log V| < 1/τ, which at τ ≈ 38 is about ±0.026.
- Figure 6 caption: "so a design can lie above the line here and still be inside the surface." Correct, but it means these planes cannot be read as frontier plots in the way my field draws them; the line is a conditional slice and the scatter is marginal. I would not have drawn them.
- Figure 7: the chart shows essentially no designs above log V = 0 for the K = 3 fit, whereas Figure 4 sets τ so that a tenth of designs are above V = 1 at K = 1. Either the K = 3 fit uses a different temperature or the exceedance target applies differently; the slice does not say.

7. Terms I had to guess

- "Invariant value", "invariant form": the weighted geometric mean, called invariant for its ratio-scale (homogeneity) property. I read it as an output distance function.
- "Energy differential", "level offset", "potential": log V. The potential-theory metaphor did no work for me; it is the log of a distance to the surface.
- "Reach": the (soft) support function of the data cloud in direction w_k.
- "n-lemma", "dilemma", "trilemma", "contending set": the support of a facet normal and its cardinality, that is, which metrics have nonzero multipliers on that facet.
- "Cell", "term share": the set of units whose maximising facet is term k, and its fraction among near-frontier units.
- "Chart": the change of coordinates (Z, log V); not a figure.
- "Fit temperature" and "term temperature": the sharpness of the soft maximum over designs (τ) and of the soft maximum over facets (κ).
- "Primary regime (aggressive controller gains)" and "stock (stock gains)": two sampling regimes of the design space differing in the admissible range of Kp and Kd; I guessed that each regime is a separate table and a separate fit.
- "MoP", "MoE": measure of performance and measure of effectiveness, systems-engineering usage.
- "Effective sample size": the Kish quantity 1/Σ p_i² of the softmax weights p.
- "Derived." at the head of each box: that the statement is produced by the paper's own lemma script rather than cited.
- Hypothesis codes A1 to A8, F5, F10, F12, I5, I6, I8, I9, I14 to I16, R1, R2: assumptions, facts about attainment of extrema, imported identities or inequalities, regularity conditions. R2 (γ > 0) I took to be a positive assignment margin, the gap between a row's best and second-best term score.
- "GGP-compatible", "glossary's participation threshold": generalised geometric programming; a documented cutoff of 0.001.
- "Stopping declaration": the statement a run prints when the three levels of guarantee are or are not met.

8. Would I read the whole paper

Yes, and selectively: Section 2 with Table 1 and Appendix G first, then Section 6.3, Section 9 and Appendix D.16. The reason is not that the mathematics is new to me; most of the K = 1 material I could have written on a board, and the K > 1 algorithm is one I have seen under other names. The reason is that the statements are unusually careful about what they do not claim, the three-object distinction in Definition 1 is the distinction my field spends effort teaching, and the case study is a real engineering table with known physics where the method's readings can be checked. I want to see whether the prior-art table knows multiplicative DEA, the hyperbolic measure, Aigner-Chu and partial frontiers; if it does and says plainly how this differs, the paper earns its claim of a method; if it does not, my review will supply the references and ask the authors to describe the work as a parsimonious, temperature-smoothed piecewise log-linear envelopment, which is a perfectly respectable thing to have built. The collinearity swap between energy balance and power margin across the two regimes, visible in the authors' own Figure 9, is the point I would press hardest.
