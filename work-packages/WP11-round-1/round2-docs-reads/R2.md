## 1. What the site is about

A Python package, `hyperbolic_trade_space`, that learns the shape of a design trade space from a table of already-evaluated designs. You give it designs scored on several metrics (all positive, larger is better) and it fits a "frontier" model made of one or more monomial terms (products of powers of the metrics). Each term is one trade-off: its positive exponents name which metrics compete (the "contending set", called an n-lemma: dilemma, trilemma, 5-lemma...), and the exponent ratios are the exchange rates (elasticities). It also places each design relative to the fitted surface (log V, negative inside, positive beyond) and reports gaps in coverage. The pitch is "PCA explains variance; this explains tensions", and it is packaged as a scikit-learn estimator (fit / transform / inverse_transform / score_samples), which is what made it approachable for me. The worked example is a cubesat trade study: 706 feasible designs, 8 attributes (settling time, pointing error, total mass, control effort, power margin, energy balance, peak slew power, depth of discharge), conditioned to metrics with a LogAffinePreconditioner.

## 6. What I chose for K and why

I chose K = 3 for the primary cubesat table.

Following the Fitting page: temperature fixed at the K=1 value (tau 38.1), compare mean_slack_, keep adding terms only while the resampled terms stay stable.

- Mean slack at fixed tau: 0.243 → 0.132 → 0.088 → 0.070. The second term removes 0.112, the third 0.044, the fourth 0.018. The page says this number falls by construction, so it only tells me where the returns shrink; the step from three to four is the first clearly small one.
- Stability over ten bootstrap resamples: at K=3 the same three families of terms kept coming back: (a) a term dominated by peak slew power (0.58–0.61) with pointing error, total mass, settling time and power margin, holding about 70 percent of the near-frontier share, 10/10 resamples; (b) a control-effort / settling-time / energy-balance term, 10/10 (with depth of discharge in or out); (c) the one-term contending set (total mass, energy balance, pointing error, depth of discharge, settling time), 7/10. At K=4, in 6 of 10 resamples two of the four terms had the identical participating set, and on the full table terms 1 and 4 were the same 4-lemma. That is what the Fitting page calls a splinter: the fourth term is fitting a few designs that already belong to another term.
- Each K=3 cell at fixed tau has enough near-frontier designs (27, 17, 118, all above the ten the Tensions page requires) for a per-term tension report with an association reading. At K=2 the small term sits exactly at 10 and holds 7 percent of the share.
- The three terms read as engineering stories I recognise: an agility term (peak slew power against pointing error and mass), a control term (effort and settling time against energy balance) and the mass/energy/battery-depth term that the single-term fit found. The one-term set survives intact as one of the three (the script's "term 2: shares 5 of 5"), so K=3 refines K=1 rather than contradicting it.

What makes me hold this loosely: family (c) was missing in 3 of 10 resamples, where it blended with the control term; the smallest term's share wandered between 0.06 and 0.16; and at fixed tau the fraction of designs above V=1 rises to 0.26, far from the 0.10 target the temperature was chosen for, which the Fitting page warns is normal with several terms but which I cannot judge. K=2 is the cautious alternative (both its terms recur in every resample), at the cost of lumping the mass/battery trade-off into the control term and leaving one cell with barely enough near-frontier designs to report on.

## 7. What I believe the method does and does not do

Does: from a table of positive larger-is-better metrics, fits a piecewise-flat (in log space) frontier made of K monomial terms; for each term names the metrics whose exponents are positive (the contending set) and their exchange rates (elasticities); assigns each design to a term (cell) and gives it a signed log distance to the surface (score_samples); reports near-frontier correlation and principal directions per term, chaining with PCA; gives a reversible chart (transform / inverse_transform); records how the solver stopped and a local-optimality certificate. It is sensitive to the conditioning (tau is in units of the conditioned log-spread) and to the regime of the data (stock vs aggressive gains gave different sets).

Does not: choose K for you (the glossary is explicit: "the analyst's choice, never selected by the fit"); explain tensions physically ("observations of the fit, not explanations"); claim causality from association (the closure note says negative correlation near the surface is partly mechanical); find trade-offs the sampled table never exercised; say anything about predictions outside the table (the Termination page says to check that on held-out evaluations); handle requirements/thresholds inside the fit (they are read after). The certificate is about a local optimum of the solver, not about the data being right. I did not learn from these pages how to pick the band or temperature robustly for several terms, or what "ilr" versus "alr" would change.
